Section 1 · Hidden Quality
Fig 12.1

The Market for Lemons: When Do Peaches Survive?

Buyers cannot tell a peach from a lemon, so they offer the average value \(\mathbb{E}[WTP] = 1{,}200 + 1{,}200p\), where \(p\) is the fraction of peaches. Peach sellers will only sell if that average clears their $2,000 reserve price. Slide the peach fraction: above the knife-edge \(p^{*} = 2/3\) the market pools and both types trade; below it, the average is too low, peaches exit, and only lemons are left.

Fraction of peaches p: 0.50
Information can destroy a market. Adverse selection means a market that would function perfectly under full information can collapse: once the average price buyers will pay falls below the peach seller’s reserve, good cars exit, the pool worsens, and only lemons trade. The $400 gain from every peach sale is lost.
Section 2 · Credible Signals
Fig 12.2

Spence Signalling: The Separating Range

High-productivity workers earn \(y_H = \$100k\), low types \(y_L = \$40k\); education costs the high type \(c_H = s\) but the low type \(c_L = 3s\) (single crossing). An employer pays the high wage only above an education threshold \(s^{*}\). Slide it: the threshold separates the types only when it is high enough that the low type will not mimic (\(s^{*} \ge 20\)) yet low enough that the high type still bothers (\(s^{*} \le 60\)). The green band is the whole separating range; \(s^{*} = 20\) is the least-wasteful one.

Education threshold s*: 20
Single-crossing is the whole trick. A signal separates types only when it is cheaper for the high type, so the low type will not mimic it. Because education adds no productivity in this model, any positive signalling level is pure social waste, a cost paid solely to prove who you are.
Section 3 · Moral Hazard & Incentives
Fig 12.3

The Incentive-Insurance Trade-off

Under moral hazard the principal ties pay to output with a linear contract \(w=\alpha+\beta\pi\). The optimal performance weight is \(\beta^{*}=\dfrac{1}{1+r\sigma^{2}}\), where \(r\) is the agent’s risk aversion and \(\sigma^{2}\) the noise in output. A higher \(\beta\) sharpens incentives but loads more risk onto the agent. Slide the noise and the risk aversion to watch the optimal contract move between sharp incentives and flat insurance.

Output noise σ²: 1.0
Risk aversion r: 1.0
Pay for what effort controls. When output closely tracks effort (low noise, low risk aversion) the optimal contract is steep performance pay, a salesperson on commission. When output is noisy or the agent is very risk-averse, extra performance weight mostly transfers risk rather than sharpening effort, so pay flattens toward a salary. That is the incentive-insurance trade-off in one curve.
Section 4 · When Markets Unravel
Fig 12.4

Market Unravelling, Round by Round

With more than two quality tiers, adverse selection cascades. Three types of used car are offered: Lemons (50%, seller reserve $700), Mediums (30%, reserve $1,500) and Peaches (20%, reserve $3,000). Buyers pay the average value of whatever remains in the pool. Advance the rounds: each time the average price falls below a type’s reserve, that type withdraws, the pool worsens, and the price falls further, until only lemons trade.

Round: 0
Good goods stop trading. Adverse selection is not just “more bad cars than good”: it is a chain reaction that can wipe the high-quality market out entirely. Each exit lowers the average buyers will pay, which forces the next-best type out, even though buyers and those sellers would both gain from trade. This is the deeper welfare cost, the destruction of whole markets under asymmetric information.
Fig 12.5

Credit Rationing: Why Banks Cap the Rate

A bank lends $1,000 to safe borrowers (succeed 90% of the time) and risky ones (succeed 50%), an even mix it cannot tell apart. Raising the interest rate looks like it should raise the bank’s return, but past a threshold the safe borrowers, whose projects no longer clear the higher repayment, drop out, leaving only the risky pool. The bank’s expected return then falls. Slide the rate to find the peak: the bank stops there and rations credit rather than raise the rate further.

Interest rate r: 40%
Adverse selection sets the price. Because the pool of borrowers gets worse as the rate rises, the interest rate is not a free lever: past the point where safe borrowers exit, a higher rate means a worse pool and a lower expected return. So the bank holds the rate at that peak and turns some borrowers away, credit rationing, rather than let the market clear on price.
