Fig 2.4, ten label treatments

Layout is held at the ratio split you picked, so the only thing changing here is how the two curve labels are placed. Try each with "Price of X down" at a high magnitude, which is the state that crowds the corner hardest.

Fixed in all ten

  • Label type is 15px, up from 13px. Tick numbers are 14px, up from 9px on the live site.
  • The Y axis title is no longer clipped. The board reserves explicit headroom in pixels for it.
  • The X axis title is out of the tick lane. It used to sit next to the last tick number; axis titles now sit past the end of each arrow in room reserved for them.
  • Labels are real DOM elements now, measured before they are placed. Placing them as chart text meant the code never knew how wide a label actually was, which is why they kept meeting the axis.
  1. 11Opaque plateThe label carries its own white background and a thin border in its curve colour, so whatever is behind it stops mattering. Placed on the normal to its curve, with the side chosen by measured clearance.Simplest thing that cannot be overlapped. Costs a small rectangle per label.
  2. 12Halo, no boxNo rectangle at all. The glyphs are ringed in the paper colour so the label stays readable where it crosses a line, without adding another box to a figure that already has several.Lightest on the eye. Reads slightly less crisply where it sits directly on a thick curve.
  3. 13Relaxation (the d3 idea)The label starts on the normal and is then pushed out of every obstacle it touches, over 220 iterations, with a weak spring back to its anchor. Obstacles are both curves, both axes, every tick number box and the other label.This is the one you described. It reacts to the real rendered box, but the result depends on the starting state, so it is not perfectly reproducible.
  4. 14Out to the right marginLabels leave the plot entirely and stack in the margin, each joined to its curve by a leader. Collision stops being a thing that has to be solved.Guaranteed clean. Costs horizontal room and makes the eye travel.
  5. 15At the end of the lineEach label sits just past where its own curve stops, the way a time series is labelled. No leader needed because the label continues the line.Very natural to read. Only works where the curve ends inside the frame.
  6. 16Set into the curveThe plate sits on the line and masks the segment underneath, the way a contour map breaks a contour to fit its number.Unambiguous about which line it belongs to. Interrupts the curve, which matters more on a curve than on a straight budget line.
  7. 17One corner legendNo per-curve labels at all. A single block in the corner the data never reaches, with a line swatch for each series.Nothing can collide with anything. The reader has to match colour to line themselves.
  8. 18Compass searchEight directions around the anchor times three distances. Every candidate is scored on measured clearance from curves, axes, tick numbers, other labels and the frame edge. Best score wins.Same inputs always give the same placement, which the relaxation cannot promise. Easier to reason about when a figure looks wrong.
  9. 19Right-hand railA rail inside the figure but outside the plot. Labels are ordered by where their curve sits vertically and nudged apart, so their order always matches the order of the lines.Predictable and tidy. Like the margin, it spends width.
  10. 20Plate plus compassThe opaque plate from 11 placed by the scoring from 18, with a slightly finer search: twelve directions times four distances.My pick for production: it cannot be overlapped, it cannot drift, and it degrades gracefully when the plot is crowded.
