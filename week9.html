Non-Linear Pricing, Natural Monopoly & Game Theory

Week 9 extends the monopoly pricing of Week 8 in three directions: non-linear (two-part) pricing, the natural-monopoly regulatory dilemma, and a first course in game theory.

Chapter Sections

Topics

Linear pricing sets \(T(q)=p\cdot q\): the total bill is exactly proportional to quantity, so the per-unit price is constant. Non-linear pricing makes \(T(q)/q\) vary with quantity, so the schedule can track the shape of the demand curve more closely than any single flat price.

A coffee shop that charges $1.60 for an 8 oz cup ($0.20/oz) and $2.00 for 16 oz ($0.125/oz) is pricing non-linearly: the larger cup is not twice the price. Charging high prices for early units (where willingness to pay is highest) and lower prices for later units extracts more total surplus than a flat price.

Course ThreadWeek 8 showed a uniform monopolist leaves a triangle of consumer surplus on the table and destroys a triangle of deadweight loss. Non-linear pricing is the firm’s way to reach into that surplus without observing each buyer’s exact willingness to pay.

A two-part tariff splits the price into a fixed fee \(F\) to gain access and a per-unit charge \(p\) for each unit consumed: \(T(q)=F+p\cdot q\). The optimal rule is two steps: set \(p^{*}=MC\) (this maximises the quantity sold and therefore the total surplus available), then set \(F^{*}=CS(p=MC)\) (this transfers that entire surplus to the firm as a lump sum).

For linear demand this exactly doubles uniform monopoly profit: the monopoly profit rectangle is exactly half the consumer-surplus triangle, and two-part pricing captures both halves. Amazon Prime is the textbook example: a fixed annual fee (\(F\approx\$139\)) plus a $0 per-order shipping charge (\(p=MC\)) that encourages maximum usage.

Common Trap“Setting \(p=0\) maximises two-part profit.” No: \(p<MC\) forces the firm to lose money on every unit sold, and that loss more than offsets the larger fixed fee. The optimum is \(p^{*}=MC\), not zero.

When consumers differ and types are unobservable, the firm chooses between a type-specific fee (if types are observable), a uniform fee that retains both types, or excluding low-demand consumers. With the gym example (Fitness Nuts \(CS=\$162\), Casual Exercisers \(CS=\$50\)), observing types is most profitable ($212); a uniform fee that keeps both types earns $100 but leaves the high type an information rent of $112.

Business InsightThis is why firms invest so heavily in loyalty programmes, usage data, and tiered memberships (Gold/Silver/Basic): any information that helps identify high-demand users lets the firm shift toward the more profitable type-specific pricing.

A natural monopoly is a market where average cost declines over the entire relevant range of output, so a single firm supplies at lower total cost than two or more. The cause is the cost structure: large fixed cost \(FC\) plus low marginal cost \(c\) gives \(AC=FC/Q+c>c=MC\) for all \(Q>0\). Because \(MC<AC\) everywhere, any price equal to marginal cost lies below average cost, so the firm cannot survive on MC pricing without a subsidy.

This is the regulator’s core dilemma. Electricity grids, water pipes, broadband backbones, railway track, and gas pipelines all share enormous fixed infrastructure and near-zero marginal cost per extra user.

Not a Market FailureA natural monopoly is not a failure in the usual sense: it is a consequence of the technology. It is genuinely cheaper for society to have one firm operate the infrastructure than two firms each building their own. The problem is what to do with that monopolist once it exists.

When the regulator permits a natural monopoly to charge different prices to different groups, profits from a high-price group can cover losses on a low-price group with no government subsidy. A railway with \(MC=\$20\) and \(FC=\$4{,}000\) can charge business travellers (inelastic) \(P_B=\$110,\,Q_B=45\), earning variable profit \((110-20)(45)=\$4{,}050\); leisure travellers pay near \(MC=\$20\) at zero variable profit. The business profit covers the fixed cost.

Third-Degree, In a Regulatory SuitThis is third-degree price discrimination applied in a regulatory context: the regulator requires above-cost pricing for one group precisely so the firm can afford near-MC pricing for another. The business traveller effectively pays for the network’s fixed infrastructure.

In competition and monopoly each agent optimises taking prices as given. That breaks down in oligopoly, where each firm’s profit depends directly on rivals’ choices. A game has three elements: players, strategies, and payoffs. A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where each player’s choice is a best response to the other’s, so no one can gain by deviating unilaterally. A dominant strategy is a best response to every strategy of the opponent.

In the prisoner’s dilemma, Confess is dominant for both, so the unique Nash equilibrium is (Confess, Confess) = \((-3,-3)\) even though (Silent, Silent) = \((-2,-2)\) is better for both. Individual rationality produces a collectively irrational outcome. The same structure drives price wars, advertising wars, arms races, and the tragedy of the commons.

The Circle MethodFor each column, underline the row player’s best payoff; for each row, underline the column player’s best payoff. Any cell with both payoffs underlined is a Nash equilibrium. This best-response test is the workhorse for the whole of Weeks 9–10.

Not every strategic game is a prisoner’s dilemma. A coordination game has multiple Nash equilibria, and the challenge is that players must coordinate on the same one without being able to communicate. In the technology-standard game, (Standard A, Standard A) = \((8,8)\) and (Standard B, Standard B) = \((6,6)\) are both Nash equilibria; A Pareto-dominates B, but if each firm expects the other to pick B, B is a best response too.

Coordination FailureWhen the Pareto-superior equilibrium is not reached, we call it coordination failure: VHS vs Betamax, QWERTY vs Dvorak, AC vs DC current. Once a standard achieves critical mass the network effect locks it in, so “first-mover advantage” can decide which equilibrium the market coordinates on.

Quick Reference

Game Theory in Four Ideas

Nash Equilibrium
A strategy profile where each player’s choice is a best response to the other’s. No one gains by deviating alone. It is self-enforcing.
Dominant Strategy
A best response to every strategy of the opponent. A rational player always plays it. Every dominant-strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium.
Prisoner’s Dilemma
One Nash equilibrium, Pareto-dominated by the cooperative outcome. Individual rationality yields a collectively bad result.
Coordination Game
Multiple Nash equilibria. The problem is selecting the good one, not escaping a bad one. Communication or a focal point helps.

Exam Prep

Five Common Mistakes

Watch out for these

  • Setting the two-part usage charge below MC. The optimum is \(p^{*}=MC\), not \(p=0\). A below-cost usage charge loses money on every unit and shrinks total profit.
  • Forgetting the fixed fee equals CS at \(p=MC\), not CS at the monopoly price. First set \(p=MC\) so the surplus pie is as large as possible, then measure the consumer surplus you will take as \(F\).
  • Claiming MC pricing is always best for a natural monopoly. It maximises total surplus but loses money because \(MC<AC\); without a subsidy the firm exits. AC pricing is the usual practical choice.
  • Calling (Silent, Silent) a Nash equilibrium. It is Pareto-superior but not stable: each player can improve by deviating to Confess, so it is not a Nash equilibrium.
  • Assuming a Nash equilibrium is efficient. A Nash equilibrium only means no unilateral deviation helps. The prisoner’s dilemma shows a Nash outcome can be worse for everyone than an unreachable cooperative one.
