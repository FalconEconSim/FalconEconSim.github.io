Week 11 · Intermediate Microeconomics

Public Goods & Externalities

Prof. Naveen Sunder

Interactive Visualizations Homework
Note: This page is a structured summary of the Week 11 chapter. It covers the two central reasons markets fail, public goods and externalities, and the policy instruments (property rights, Pigouvian taxes, and cap-and-trade) that can restore efficiency. The unifying theme: when private prices diverge from social costs and benefits, markets fail.

Learning Objectives

  • LO2Analyse private provision of a public good as a game; show how partial contributions turn the prisoner’s dilemma into a coordination game.

Chapter Sections

Topics

A pure public good is non-excludable (once provided, no one can be prevented from consuming it) and non-rival (one person’s use does not reduce availability, so the marginal cost of serving an extra consumer is zero). National defence and broadcast radio are the classic examples.

Non-excludability creates the free-rider problem: because no one can be charged, each person waits for someone else to pay and enjoys the benefit for free. In the extreme, the good is never provided even though everyone would benefit. This is the public-goods version of the prisoner’s dilemma: individual rationality produces collective irrationality.

Non-Rival Does Not Mean FreeNon-rivalry means the marginal cost of serving an extra consumer is zero, not that the good costs nothing to produce. National defence is non-rival but costs hundreds of billions. Once it exists everyone should have access at zero marginal charge, but the fixed cost must still be covered, usually through taxation.

Provision is socially desirable when total willingness to pay exceeds cost: \(r_A+r_B>c\), even if no single person’s WTP exceeds cost alone. In the binary “pay the full cost or not” game, Free-ride strictly dominates Contribute, so the unique Nash equilibrium is (Free-ride, Free-ride) and the good is not provided.

Allowing partial contributions transforms the strategic structure from a prisoner’s dilemma into a coordination game with two Nash equilibria: the efficient one (both contribute their share) and the zero-provision one. The challenge shifts from “will the good be provided?” to “can we coordinate on the good equilibrium?”

Kickstarter’s All-or-Nothing RuleCrowdfunding implements exactly this structure: backers are only charged if the project reaches its funding target. A pledge costs nothing unless the target is met, so contributing becomes weakly dominant for anyone who values the project. The all-or-nothing threshold coordinates expectations on the efficient equilibrium.

An externality is a cost or benefit imposed on a third party not involved in the transaction and not reflected in market prices. A negative externality (factory pollution) means \(SMC=PMC+MEC>PMC\), so the market overproduces. A positive externality (vaccination, education) means \(SMB=MB+MEB>MB\), so the market underproduces.

The market equates \(PMC=MB\) at \(Q^{E}\), but the social optimum requires \(SMC=MB\) at \(Q^{*}\). Every unit between \(Q^{*}\) and \(Q^{E}\) has a social cost exceeding its social benefit: that gap is the deadweight loss, \(DWL=\tfrac12(Q^{E}-Q^{*})\times[SMC(Q^{E})-MB(Q^{E})]\).

Don’t Confuse MEC with SMCThe DWL triangle uses the vertical distance between SMC and MB at \(Q^{E}\), not the area under the MEC curve alone. A common error is to compute the triangle from MEC by itself; always measure the gap between the social-cost and demand curves.

The Coase theorem states that if property rights are well-defined, transaction costs are zero, and parties can bargain freely, the market reaches an efficient allocation regardless of who holds the right. The polluter and the victim bargain to the same efficient pollution level \(S^{*}\); only the distribution of gains changes, not efficiency.

In practice the three conditions are routinely violated: high transaction costs when millions are affected, hold-up after sunk investments, information asymmetry about true costs and damages, and free-riding among victims. When Coasian bargaining is impractical (global carbon emissions), government intervention is the only path to the optimum.

When Coase Works, and When It Doesn’tA factory and a single downstream fishery with clear rights and low bargaining costs will reach the efficient outcome on their own. But when emissions affect 500 communities across three countries, transaction costs are enormous and each community free-rides: the conditions fail catastrophically, exactly like global carbon.

A Pigouvian tax sets a per-unit tax equal to the marginal external cost at the social optimum: \(t^{*}=MEC(Q^{*})\), not \(MEC(Q^{E})\). The tax shifts the effective supply curve from PMC to \(PMC+t^{*}=SMC\), moving the equilibrium to \(Q^{*}\) and eliminating the deadweight loss.

With the benchmark \(P=100-Q,\ PMC=10+Q,\ MEC=20\): the social optimum is \(Q^{*}=35\), and \(t^{*}=20\) restores it exactly, wiping out the $100 DWL. When MEC is constant, \(MEC(Q^{*})=MEC(Q^{E})\), but when MEC depends on \(Q\) you must first solve for \(Q^{*}\), then evaluate MEC there.

The Tax Does Not Stop Pollution EntirelyThe Pigouvian tax internalises the external cost so the firm faces the full social cost; it moves output to the efficient level, where some pollution remains because the marginal benefit of that output still exceeds its full social cost. Zero pollution is generally not efficient.

Under cap-and-trade, the regulator sets a total pollution cap, issues permits equal to it, and lets firms buy and sell. At equilibrium every firm equates its marginal abatement cost to the permit price: \(MAC_A(e_A^{*})=MAC_B(e_B^{*})=\lambda^{*}\). Firms with low abatement costs clean up more and sell permits; firms with high costs buy them. The environmental target is hit at minimum total cost.

With \(MAC_A=2e_A,\ MAC_B=8e_B\) and total abatement 100: equalising MACs gives \(e_A^{*}=80,\ e_B^{*}=20\), permit price \(\lambda^{*}=\$160\), total cost $8,000, versus $12,500 under equal regulation (a 36% saving). Equal regulation wastes the low-cost firm’s advantage.

Tax vs Cap: Weitzman’s RuleUse a tax (price instrument) when marginal abatement costs are steep; use a cap (quantity instrument) when marginal damages are steep, such as cliff-edge damage thresholds. Real systems often combine both: a price floor plus a tradeable cap, giving both price and quantity certainty.

Quick Reference

The Four-Quadrant Goods Taxonomy

RivalNon-rival
Excludable Private goods. Food, clothing, airline seats. Market works: the seller can price and exclude. Club goods. Netflix, toll roads, gyms. Excludable, so a firm can charge an access fee even though MC ≈ 0.
Non-excludable Common-pool resources. Fisheries, aquifers, congested roads. Tragedy of the commons: over-exploitation. Pure public goods. National defence, broadcast radio, public health. Free-rider problem: market under-provides.

Non-excludability is what makes the market fail. Non-rivalry only removes the efficiency reason to charge.

Exam Prep

Five Common Mistakes

Watch out for these

  • Thinking non-rival means the good should be free to produce. Non-rivalry sets the efficient marginal charge to zero, but the fixed cost of producing the good must still be covered.
  • Evaluating the Pigouvian tax at \(Q^{E}\) instead of \(Q^{*}\). The correct tax is \(t^{*}=MEC(Q^{*})\). With constant MEC the two coincide, but with \(MEC=bQ\) you must solve for \(Q^{*}\) first.
  • Computing the DWL from the MEC curve alone. The triangle uses the vertical gap between SMC and MB at the market quantity, not the area under MEC by itself.
  • Assuming the Coase theorem always delivers efficiency. It needs well-defined rights, zero transaction costs, and free bargaining. With many parties and high transaction costs, it fails and government action is required.
  • Believing equal regulation and cap-and-trade cost the same. Equal abatement forces high-cost firms to over-abate; equalising marginal abatement costs across firms hits the target far more cheaply.
