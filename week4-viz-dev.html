Elasticity Meter: Point vs Arc

Slide the two points along a linear demand curve. Point elasticity measures responsiveness at one location, \(e = -b\,P/Q\). Arc (midpoint) elasticity measures it between two points using base values at the midpoint. Toggle the modes and watch the two methods diverge as the gap widens. The point colour itself is the meter: red = elastic, amber = unit, blue = inelastic.

Fig 4.3 · Elasticity

Revenue Rectangle: Price vs Quantity Effect

Total revenue is the area \(P\times Q\). Raise the price and two forces fight: the price effect (green) — existing buyers pay more — and the quantity effect (red) — some buyers leave. Whichever wins decides if revenue rises or falls, and that is exactly the elasticity test. The dashed ghost rectangle marks the old revenue.

The link: price effect wins when demand is inelastic (raise price → revenue up); quantity effect wins when demand is elastic (raise price → revenue down). They tie at unit elasticity, where revenue is maximised.

Pricing Strategy Simulator

Pick an industry with its real-world empirical elasticity, choose a price change, and read the predicted revenue response. The approximation is \(\%\Delta TR \approx \%\Delta P\times(1+e_d)\). Inelastic industries (insulin, gasoline) reward price hikes; elastic ones (airlines, fashion) punish them.

Consumer Surplus Triangle

Consumer surplus (CS) is the blue triangle between the demand curve and the price line — the value buyers get above what they pay. Set a base price, then raise it: the lost surplus splits into a transfer rectangle (buyers who stay, now paying more) and an exit triangle (buyers priced out entirely). Surge Pricing Simulator

Ride-share surge pricing as a live welfare trade-off. As the surge multiplier climbs above the normal $5 fare, riders drop out, consumer surplus (blue) shrinks, and the platform's revenue (green rectangle) grows — until the elasticity of demand turns the hike against it. The dashed line marks the normal fare.

Fig 4.7 · Elasticity

Elasticity Drill

Read each market situation and classify demand as elastic (|e|>1, buyers very responsive), unit-elastic (|e|=1, revenue unchanged by a price tweak), or inelastic (|e|<1, buyers stuck). Necessities with no substitutes are inelastic; luxuries and goods with close rivals are elastic. Every miss gets a contrastive explanation.
