Oligopoly & Spatial Competition

Prof. Naveen Sunder

Section 1 · Quantity Competition
Fig 10.1

Cournot & Stackelberg on the Best-Response Diagram

Market demand is \(P = a - bQ\) with \(MC = 0\). Each firm’s reaction curve gives its profit-maximising output as a function of the rival’s. Pick a market demand to see how the reaction curves and the equilibrium shift. Then drag Firm A’s output: Firm B always best-responds along its own reaction curve (the moving dot rides B’s curve), but only where the two curves cross is A also best-responding, the Cournot–Nash equilibrium. Away from that crossing the market is not at rest, so press Watch adjustment and let the firms take turns best-responding: the staircase spirals into the Cournot point. Push A out to the Stackelberg quantity to see the first-mover advantage.

Market demand
Firm A’s output qA: 40 Moves A off its reaction curve; B keeps best-responding. Only the crossing is an equilibrium.
Commitment is power. The Stackelberg leader produces more than its Cournot quantity and earns a premium purely by moving first, forcing the follower down its reaction curve. But the commitment must be both observable and irreversible, a threat the rival does not believe changes nothing. And notice the adjustment path: from any starting point the best-response dynamics converge back to the same Cournot crossing, which is what makes it a stable equilibrium.
Section 2 · Location Competition
Fig 10.2

The Hotelling Beach: Why Competitors Cluster

Two vendors sit on a one-mile beach; sunbathers are spread evenly and always walk to the nearer vendor. The dashed line is the midpoint that splits the market. Move either vendor: whoever slides toward the centre captures a bigger slice, so both keep creeping inward until they sit side by side at \(0.5\), the principle of minimum differentiation. The socially efficient spread (the quarter-points) minimises walking, but it is not a Nash equilibrium.

Vendor A location: 0.30
Vendor B location: 0.70
Minimum differentiation. Both vendors converge to the centre even though the quarter-points would minimise total travel. Each firm cares only about its own market share, not total travel cost, so private incentives and social efficiency pull in opposite directions, which is why competitors so often cluster.
Section 3 · How Many Firms?
Fig 10.3

n-Firm Cournot: From Monopoly to Competition

With \(n\) identical firms and \(P = 120 - Q,\ MC = 0\), each firm produces \(q^{*}=\tfrac{120}{n+1}\) and the market price is \(P^{*}=\tfrac{120}{n+1}\). Slide the number of firms: one firm is the monopoly (\(P=\$60\)), two is the Cournot duopoly ($40), and as \(n\) grows the price slides down toward marginal cost. Cournot nests monopoly and perfect competition as its two limiting cases.

Number of firms n: 2
Competition is a limit, not a switch. The same model spans the whole spectrum: market power fades smoothly as firms are added, and price approaches marginal cost only as \(n\to\infty\). This is why the number of competitors, not just their existence, shapes how close a market gets to the efficient outcome.
Section 4 · The Strategic Variable Matters
Fig 10.4

Four Models, One Market

The same duopoly (\(P = 120 - Q,\ MC = 0\)) yields four different answers depending on what firms choose and when. Cournot firms pick quantities at once; a Stackelberg leader commits first; colluding firms split the monopoly output; Bertrand firms set prices and compete it away to marginal cost. Switch between models to see total output, price, and industry profit move, with the profit rectangle shaded on the demand diagram.

Not just how many firms, but how they compete. Collusion reproduces the monopoly ($3,600 industry profit); two Bertrand price-setters wipe profit out entirely ($0); Cournot and Stackelberg sit in between. The strategic variable, quantity versus price, and the timing, simultaneous versus sequential, matter as much as the number of competitors.
Fig 10.5

Bertrand: The Incentive to Undercut

With identical products buyers go to whoever is cheapest. Market demand is \(Q = 120 - P\) and \(MC = \$20\). Your profit depends on your price relative to the rival’s: price above the rival and you sell nothing; match and you split the market; undercut (while staying above cost) and you capture the whole market. Slide your price against a fixed rival price and watch your best response, always to shave just below the rival, which is what drives price down to marginal cost.

Your price: $50
Rival’s price: $60
The paradox: two firms are enough. Because the best response is always to undercut by a penny, any price above marginal cost is unstable, and price collapses to \(MC\) with zero profit, the perfectly competitive outcome from just two firms. The paradox breaks only when capacity is limited, products differ, or the game repeats.
