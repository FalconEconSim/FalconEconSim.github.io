Oligopoly & Spatial Competition

Prof. Naveen Sunder

Section 1 · Quantity Competition
Fig 10.1

Cournot & Stackelberg on the Best-Response Diagram

Market demand is \(P = 120 - Q\) with \(MC = 0\). Each firm’s reaction curve gives its profit-maximising output as a function of the rival’s. Slide Firm A’s output and watch Firm B best-respond along its reaction curve. Where the two curves cross, both are simultaneously best-responding: the Cournot–Nash equilibrium \((40, 40)\). Push A to 60 and B falls to 30: the Stackelberg point, where committing first raises A’s profit.

Firm A’s output qA: 40
Commitment is power. The Stackelberg leader produces more than its Cournot quantity and earns a premium purely by moving first, forcing the follower down its reaction curve. But the commitment must be both observable and irreversible, a threat the rival does not believe changes nothing.
Section 2 · Location Competition
Fig 10.2

The Hotelling Beach: Why Competitors Cluster

Two vendors sit on a one-mile beach; sunbathers are spread evenly and always walk to the nearer vendor. The dashed line is the midpoint that splits the market. Move either vendor: whoever slides toward the centre captures a bigger slice, so both keep creeping inward until they sit side by side at \(0.5\), the principle of minimum differentiation. The socially efficient spread (the quarter-points) minimises walking, but it is not a Nash equilibrium.

Vendor A location: 0.30
Vendor B location: 0.70
Minimum differentiation. Both vendors converge to the centre even though the quarter-points would minimise total travel. Each firm cares only about its own market share, not total travel cost, so private incentives and social efficiency pull in opposite directions, which is why competitors so often cluster.
Section 3 · How Many Firms?
Fig 10.3

n-Firm Cournot: From Monopoly to Competition

With \(n\) identical firms and \(P = 120 - Q,\ MC = 0\), each firm produces \(q^{*}=\tfrac{120}{n+1}\) and the market price is \(P^{*}=\tfrac{120}{n+1}\). Slide the number of firms: one firm is the monopoly (\(P=\$60\)), two is the Cournot duopoly ($40), and as \(n\) grows the price slides down toward marginal cost. Cournot nests monopoly and perfect competition as its two limiting cases.

Number of firms n: 2
Competition is a limit, not a switch. The same model spans the whole spectrum: market power fades smoothly as firms are added, and price approaches marginal cost only as \(n\to\infty\). This is why the number of competitors, not just their existence, shapes how close a market gets to the efficient outcome.
