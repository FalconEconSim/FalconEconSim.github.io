Quantity Competition
Fig 10.1

Cournot & Stackelberg on the Best-Response Diagram

Market demand is \(P = a - bQ\) with \(MC = 0\). Each firm’s reaction curve gives its profit-maximizing output as a function of the rival’s. Firm B always best-responds along its own reaction curve, but only where the two curves cross is A also best-responding, the Cournot-Nash equilibrium. Away from that crossing the market is not at rest: the firms take turns best-responding and the staircase spirals into the Cournot point. A Stackelberg leader instead commits first and picks the point on B’s curve it likes best, where one of its iso-profit curves just touches B’s curve. In Fig 10.1 below you draw B’s reaction curve and find that point; afterwards you can change the market demand, move A’s output and watch the firms adjust.

Commitment is power. The Stackelberg leader produces more than its Cournot quantity and earns a premium purely by moving first, forcing the follower down its reaction curve. But the commitment must be both observable and irreversible, a threat the rival does not believe changes nothing. And notice the adjustment path: from any starting point the best-response dynamics converge back to the same Cournot crossing, which is what makes it a stable equilibrium.
Location Competition
Fig 10.2

The Hotelling Beach: Why Competitors Cluster

Two vendors sit on a one-mile beach; sunbathers are spread evenly and always walk to the nearer vendor. The dashed line is the midpoint that splits the market. Whoever slides toward the center captures a bigger slice, so both keep creeping inward until they sit side by side at \(0.5\), the principle of minimum differentiation. In Fig 10.2 below, find vendor A’s best spot against a fixed B and predict where both end up; afterwards you can move either vendor. The socially efficient spread (the quarter-points) minimizes walking, but it is not a Nash equilibrium.

Minimum differentiation. Both vendors converge to the center even though the quarter-points would minimize total travel. Each firm cares only about its own market share, not total travel cost, so private incentives and social efficiency pull in opposite directions, which is why competitors so often cluster.
How Many Firms?
Fig 10.3

n-Firm Cournot: From Monopoly to Competition

With \(n\) identical firms and \(P = 120 - Q,\ MC = 0\), each firm produces \(q^{*}=\tfrac{120}{n+1}\) and the market price is \(P^{*}=\tfrac{120}{n+1}\). One firm is the monopoly (\(P=\$60\)), two is the Cournot duopoly ($40), and as \(n\) grows the price slides down toward marginal cost. Cournot nests monopoly and perfect competition as its two limiting cases. In Fig 10.3 below, predict the price with one firm, with several, and where it heads as more firms enter.

Competition is a limit, not a switch. The same model spans the whole spectrum: market power fades smoothly as firms are added, and price approaches marginal cost only as \(n\to\infty\). This is why the number of competitors, not just their existence, shapes how close a market gets to the efficient outcome.
The Strategic Variable Matters
Fig 10.4

Four Models, One Market

The same duopoly (\(P = 120 - Q,\ MC = 0\)) yields four different answers depending on what firms choose and when. Cournot firms pick quantities at once; a Stackelberg leader commits first; colluding firms split the monopoly output; Bertrand firms set prices and compete it away to marginal cost. In Fig 10.4 below, place all four on the demand curve; afterwards pick a model to see total output, price and industry profit worked out step by step, with profit, consumer surplus and deadweight loss shaded on the demand diagram.

Not just how many firms, but how they compete. Collusion reproduces the monopoly ($3,600 industry profit); two Bertrand price-setters wipe profit out entirely ($0); Cournot and Stackelberg sit in between. The strategic variable, quantity versus price, and the timing, simultaneous versus sequential, matter as much as the number of competitors.

Bertrand: The Incentive to Undercut

With identical products buyers go to whoever is cheapest. Market demand is \(Q = 120 - P\) and \(MC = \$20\). Your profit depends on your price relative to the rival’s: price above the rival and you sell nothing; match and you split the market; undercut (while staying above cost) and you capture the whole market. Your best response to a fixed rival price is to shave just below it (or, if the rival is above the monopoly price of $70, simply charge $70), which is what drives price down to marginal cost. In Fig 10.5 below, draw your profit at every price and pick your best one; afterwards you can move both prices or start a price war.

The paradox: two firms are enough. Because the best response is always to undercut by a penny, any price above marginal cost is unstable, and price collapses to \(MC\) with zero profit, the perfectly competitive outcome from just two firms. The paradox breaks only when capacity is limited, products differ, or the game repeats.
