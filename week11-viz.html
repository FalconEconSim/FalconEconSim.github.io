Section 1 · The Externality Wedge
Fig 11.1

Negative Externality & the Pigouvian Tax

Demand is \(P = 100 - Q\) and private marginal cost is \(PMC = 10 + Q\). Each unit also dumps a marginal external cost \(MEC\) on third parties, so social marginal cost is \(SMC = PMC + MEC\). The market produces where \(PMC = MB\) (too much); the optimum is where \(SMC = MB\). The red triangle is the deadweight loss. Apply the Pigouvian tax \(t^{*} = MEC\) to lift private cost onto the social-cost curve and erase it.

Marginal external cost MEC: $20
Internalise, don’t ban. The Pigouvian tax (t^{*}=MEC(Q^{*})) lifts private cost onto the social-cost curve, moving output to the efficient (Q^{*}) and erasing the deadweight loss. Note it does not stop the activity: some pollution remains, because the efficient level is where marginal benefit equals full social cost, not zero.
Section 2 · Least-Cost Abatement
Fig 11.2

Cap-and-Trade: Equalising Marginal Abatement Cost

Two firms must together abate a total amount of pollution. Firm A cleans up cheaply (\(MAC_A = 2e_A\)); Firm B is expensive (\(MAC_B = 8e_B\)). A permit market makes every firm abate up to the same permit price, so the low-cost firm does more of the work. The result meets the target at least cost, far below forcing both firms to abate equally. Slide the total cap to see the permit price and the saving move.

Total abatement required: 100
Let the cheap firm clean up. Equalising marginal abatement cost across firms hits the pollution target at least total cost. Forcing every firm to abate the same amount wastes the low-cost firm’s advantage, here trading lets Firm A do most of the work and cuts total cost by 36%.
Section 3 · When the Spillover Is Positive
Fig 11.3

Positive Externality & the Pigouvian Subsidy

Some goods (vaccination, education, a new user joining a network) confer a marginal external benefit \(MEB\) on third parties, so social marginal benefit \(MSB = MB + MEB\) lies above private demand. The market equates \(MB = MC\) and under-produces; the optimum is where \(MSB = MC\). The red triangle is the deadweight loss from too little activity, and a Pigouvian subsidy \(s^{*}=MEB\) closes it. Slide the external benefit and toggle the subsidy.

Marginal external benefit MEB: $20
Subsidise the spillover. A positive externality is the mirror image of a negative one: the market does too little, not too much, so the fix is a subsidy, not a tax. Setting \(s^{*}=MEB(Q^{*})\) pays for the benefit conferred on others and moves output up to the efficient level, which is why governments subsidise vaccines, schooling, and R&D.
Section 4 · Public Goods & Bargaining
Fig 11.4

Public Goods & the Free-Rider Problem

A public good (a neighbourhood streetlight, a shared security guard) is worth providing when the group’s total willingness to pay exceeds its cost, even if no single person values it enough to fund it alone. But because no one can be excluded, each person waits for someone else to pay. Slide the group size and how much each member values the good against a fixed \(\$300\) cost: watch when provision is socially efficient, yet still fails because everyone free-rides.

Group size N: 3
Value per person: $120
Efficient, yet unprovided. Whenever total value exceeds the cost, providing the good is efficient, but each individual’s dominant move is to free-ride and hope the others pay. Non-excludability, not the cost, is what makes the market fail: the good is under-provided even though everyone would gain. Fixes coordinate contributions (an all-or-nothing crowdfunding threshold) or fund it through taxes.
Fig 11.5

The Coase Theorem: Bargaining to Efficiency

A factory’s marginal benefit from polluting falls as it pollutes more (\(MB = 100 - 2S\)); the marginal damage to a neighbour rises (\(MD = 2S\)). The efficient level is where they cross, \(S^{*}=25\). Coase’s insight: if property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, the two parties reach \(S^{*}\) regardless of who holds the right, only the split of the gains changes. Set the starting point by who owns the right and slide the pollution level to see the bargaining range close on \(S^{*}\).

Pollution level S: 25
Rights decide who pays, not the outcome. Start from the polluter’s right (high pollution) and the neighbour pays it to cut back; start from the neighbour’s right (zero pollution) and the polluter pays to be allowed some, either way they meet at \(S^{*}=25\). Efficiency does not depend on the allocation of rights, only on the ability to bargain, which is exactly why Coase fails when transaction costs are high or parties are many.
