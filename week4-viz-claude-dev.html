Elasticity & Consumer Welfare

Prof. Naveen Sunder

How sharply does quantity respond to price, and who gains or loses when the price moves? We measure elasticity two ways, paint it along a demand curve, watch it decide whether a price change grows or shrinks revenue, and read consumer surplus straight off the diagram. Wide frames for the demand readouts, square frames for the surplus and revenue rectangles.

Section 1 · Measuring elasticity
Fig E.1Elasticity Meter: Point vs. Arc

Two points on a linear demand curve, A and B. Point elasticity measures responsiveness at one location, e = −b·P/Q. Arc (midpoint) elasticity measures it between two points with the midpoint formula. Slide A and B and toggle the mode. The colour tracks the value (red = elastic, amber = unit, blue = inelastic).

Mode: iPoint elasticity is the exact value at one spot. Arc elasticity averages over the A to B interval, so the two diverge as A and B move apart.
Point A price: $25
Point B price: $40
Up high, elastic; down low, inelastic. On any straight-line demand curve, |e| falls from infinity at the top to zero at the bottom, passing through 1 at the midpoint.
Fig E.2Elasticity Heatmap: the Living Demand Curve

The demand curve is painted as a gradient: red where it is elastic (top), amber at the unit-elastic midpoint, blue where it is inelastic (bottom). Slide the readout point to any price; edit the demand equation to reshape the curve.

Q = − P

Section 2 · Elasticity & revenue
Fig E.3Revenue Rectangle: Price vs. Quantity Effect

Total revenue is the rectangle P×Q. Raise the price and the red strip is revenue gained from buyers who stay and pay more, while the green strip is revenue lost from buyers who leave. Which strip wins tells you whether demand is elastic or inelastic over the range.

Old price: $25
New price: $35
Demand slope b: 2.0

Pick an industry with its real-world empirical elasticity, then set a price change. The predicted revenue change uses the approximation %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP × (1 + ed). Elastic goods (|e| > 1) punish price rises; inelastic goods reward them.

Section 3 · Consumer welfare
Fig E.5Consumer-Surplus Triangle

Consumer surplus is the blue triangle between the demand curve and the price. Raise the price and CS shrinks: part becomes a rectangle transferred from buyers who stay, and part is a deadweight triangle lost as buyers exit. Toggle the worked calculation.

Base price P*: $15
New price P': $25
Fig E.6Surge-Pricing Simulator

Ride-share surge pricing. As the multiplier climbs, riders fall away, consumer surplus shrinks, and platform revenue grows, up to a point. The dashed line marks the normal fare; riders above the new price are priced out of the market.

Surge fare: $5.00
The welfare trade-off: surge pricing rations a scarce supply of rides and lifts revenue, but it transfers surplus from riders to the platform and prices some riders out entirely.
