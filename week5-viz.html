§ 5.1

Total Product & Marginal Product Animator

In Fig 5.1 below, slide points along the TP curve to where marginal product peaks and to where it reaches zero; after the reveal, slide the point and watch the tangent on TP (whose slope is MPL) and the matching MPL point in the panel under it. This makes the "MPL = slope of TP" relationship viscerally clear. Notice that as you increase the number of workers, the marginal product of labor falls. This is the Law of Diminishing Marginal Product: each additional worker adds less to total output than the previous one (assuming capital is held fixed).

S-shaped TP: TP = 1.5L2 − 0.1L3, the curve in the film and in Fig 5.1. MPL = 3L − 0.3L2 rises at first, peaks at L = 5 (MPL = 7.5), then falls and reaches zero at L = 10, where TP tops out at 50. A Cobb-Douglas TP such as Q = K0.5 · L0.5 is different: MPL falls from the first worker on (diminishing returns) but never reaches zero.
§ 5.2

Isoquant & Indifference Curve Explorer

Three production technologies give three isoquant shapes: Cobb-Douglas (smooth convex curves), Perfect Substitutes (straight lines), and Leontief / Perfect Complements (right-angle L-shapes). In Fig 5.2 below, find other bundles that make the same output as bundle A (point at any bundle to read its output). After the reveal, switch the technology, read the same map as a firm's isoquants (axes L and K) or a consumer's indifference curves (axes X and Y), and slide the point along the curve to see how MRTS changes. The math is identical; only the economic interpretation changes.

§ 5.4

Diminishing MP vs Returns to Scale

The same Cobb-Douglas function Q = KaLb can simultaneously exhibit diminishing MPL AND increasing returns to scale. Fig 5.4 below puts both on one graph. Scale only L by λ with K held fixed and output rises less than in proportion (diminishing MPL); scale both K and L by λ and, at the default a = b = 0.6, output more than doubles when λ = 2.

§ 5.5

Returns to Scale Isoquant Spacer

A ray from the origin cuts through three isoquants (Q = 1, 2, 3), equal steps in output. Under CRS the intersection points are equally spaced; under IRS they bunch up; under DRS they spread out. In Fig 5.5 below, predict where Q = 2 and Q = 3 cross the ray; after the reveal, change the exponents a and b and watch the spacing change.

§ 5.6

Input Substitution vs Technological Change

Two fundamentally different actions can change how a firm produces. Substituting inputs moves the production bundle along the existing isoquant, with technology unchanged. Improving technology shifts the entire isoquant inward: fewer inputs can now produce the same output. Fig 5.6 below asks how much capital the firm needs after each step.

§ 5.7

Consumer-Production Analogy Side-by-Side

The consumer and the firm face mathematically identical optimization problems. The consumer has an indifference curve, MRS and a budget line; the firm has an isoquant, MRTS and an isocost line. In Fig 5.7 below, pull the isocost line in until it just touches the isoquant to find the cheapest bundle; after the reveal, switch to the consumer reading to see the same picture with new names.

§ 5.9

Bakery / Factory Scenario Simulator

A bakery has a fixed number of machines (K fixed). In Fig 5.9 below, draw its new TP curve when it buys an oven; after the reveal, hire workers one at a time and watch TP, MPL, and running wage cost update. Each extra worker adds less output than the one before (diminishing MPL), although with this Cobb-Douglas bakery MPL never turns negative. Buying an oven shifts the TP curve upward.
