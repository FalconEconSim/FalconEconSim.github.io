Chapter Sections

Topics

A dominant strategy yields a strictly higher payoff than any other strategy, irrespective of the opponent’s choice. If one exists, a rational player always plays it and no reasoning about the opponent is needed. Two facts anchor the whole chapter: dominant strategies do not exist in every game, but when every player has one, the Nash equilibrium is simply the profile of dominant strategies.

Advertising Arms RaceCoca-Cola vs Pepsi: each earns more by advertising regardless of the rival (it steals share when the rival is silent, avoids losing share when the rival advertises). Advertising is dominant for both. The Nash outcome (Advertise, Advertise) is Pareto-dominated by (Don’t, Don’t): a direct parallel to the prisoner’s dilemma.

In Cournot competition firms choose quantities simultaneously, each treating the rival’s output as given. Firm A maximises \(\pi_A=(120-q_A-q_B)q_A\), giving the best-response (reaction) function \(q_A^{*}(q_B)=(120-q_B)/2\). Because A’s optimal output falls as \(q_B\) rises, quantities are strategic substitutes.

By symmetry the Cournot–Nash equilibrium (with \(P=120-Q,\,MC=0\)) is \(q_A^{*}=q_B^{*}=40,\ Q=80,\ P=\$40,\ \pi_i=\$1{,}600\). Output lies strictly between the monopoly output (60) and the competitive output (120): more firms means more output, lower price, and a more competitive outcome.

Airline Route ExampleAmerican and United set capacity months in advance, before observing the rival’s schedule: quantities are chosen simultaneously and irreversibly. Fares on two-carrier “duopoly” routes are systematically higher than on three-plus-carrier routes, exactly as the Cournot \(n\)-firm formula predicts.

Backward induction solves a sequential game by starting at the terminal nodes and working backward, choosing the payoff-maximising action at each node. The result is the subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE), a Nash equilibrium that prescribes optimal play at every subgame and so eliminates equilibria sustained by non-credible threats.

In the entry game, an incumbent’s threat to “fight” any entrant is not credible: once entry occurs, accommodating (payoff 2) beats fighting (payoff 0). Anticipating this, the entrant enters. The SPNE is (Enter, Accommodate).

Credibility Requires CommitmentA verbal threat to “fight any entrant” is empty. Credibility requires a pre-commitment (for example, building excess capacity) that makes fighting a genuine best response after entry. Backward induction is the tool that separates credible threats from bluffs.

In Stackelberg competition the leader (Firm A) commits to a quantity first and the follower (Firm B) observes and best-responds. Substituting B’s reaction function \(q_B^{*}=(120-q_A)/2\) into A’s profit gives \(\pi_A=(60-q_A/2)q_A\), maximised at \(q_A^{*}=60\). The follower then produces \(q_B^{*}=30\).

The Stackelberg outcome is \(q_A=60,\ q_B=30,\ Q=90,\ P=\$30,\ \pi_A=\$1{,}800,\ \pi_B=\$900\). The leader produces 50% more than its Cournot quantity and earns a $200 premium: the pure value of moving first. Commitment must be both observable and irreversible to confer the advantage.

Commitment Is PowerWalmart announcing store openings years ahead, Amazon building fulfilment capacity, and Apple launching an integrated ecosystem all commit credibly to a large “output” before rivals decide, exploiting the rival’s predictable best response. A capacity investment unprofitable in isolation becomes profitable when it deters rivals.

In Bertrand competition firms set prices simultaneously. With identical products, unlimited capacity, and one-shot play, the unique Nash equilibrium is \(P_A^{*}=P_B^{*}=MC\) with zero profit: either firm can capture the whole market by undercutting a cent, and that logic drives price all the way to marginal cost. Just two firms suffice to reproduce the competitive outcome, the Bertrand paradox.

The paradox rests on three assumptions that often fail: capacity constraints (a small firm cannot serve the whole market, so undercutting is limited), product differentiation (loyal customers let a firm price above MC), and repeated interaction (the threat of future price wars sustains tacit collusion).

A Calibration ToolIf a duopoly with near-identical products still prices well above MC, at least one Bertrand assumption is broken. Identifying which one (capacity? differentiation? repetition?) is more useful than memorising the paradox, and it is exactly how a regulator decides whether intervention is warranted.

Two vendors on a 1-mile beach with uniformly distributed consumers each choose a location. A consumer visits the nearer vendor, so vendor A’s share is \((a+b)/2\). Moving toward the centre raises a vendor’s share (\(\partial(\text{A’s share})/\partial a=1/2>0\)), so both keep creeping inward until they sit side by side at the middle: the principle of minimum differentiation, with unique equilibrium \(a^{*}=b^{*}=1/2\).

Why Things ClusterThe same logic explains why petrol stations cluster at one intersection, why competing political parties converge toward the centre, and why rival smartphones end up looking alike. The socially efficient outcome (vendors at the quarter-points, minimising travel) is not a Nash equilibrium.

Exam Prep

Five Common Mistakes

Watch out for these

  • Setting \(MR=MC\) on total output in Cournot. Each firm differentiates its own profit \((P-MC)q_i\) holding the rival’s quantity fixed. Treating \(Q\) as one firm’s choice gives the monopoly answer, not the Cournot one.
  • Confusing the Stackelberg leader’s and follower’s outputs. The leader commits to the larger quantity (60) and the follower best-responds with the smaller (30). Reversing them reverses the profits.
  • Thinking two firms cannot give a competitive outcome. Under Bertrand, two identical price-setters drive price to MC. Number of firms is not the only determinant of competitiveness; the strategic variable matters.
  • Treating every Nash equilibrium of a simultaneous game as an SPNE. Backward induction can eliminate a Nash equilibrium built on a non-credible threat; not every NE survives sequential play.
  • Expecting Hotelling firms to spread out efficiently. The efficient locations are the quarter-points, but the Nash equilibrium is both at the centre. Private incentives and social efficiency diverge.
