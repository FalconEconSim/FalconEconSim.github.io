Welfare, Monopoly & Price Discrimination

Prof. Naveen Sunder

Section 1 · The Welfare Framework
Fig 8.1

CS/PS Triangle Anatomy

Consumer surplus (CS) is the area under demand and above the price; producer surplus (PS) is the area above supply and below the price. Total surplus TS = CS + PS is our welfare scorecard, and it is maximised exactly at the competitive equilibrium. Drag the price slider away from P* = $12 and watch a red deadweight-loss triangle appear: mutually beneficial trades that no longer happen.

Market Price P: $12.00
Demand intercept (a): 20
Supply min price (b): 4
Key insight: Every dollar of CS is a dollar of gain to buyers. Every dollar of PS is a dollar of gain to sellers. Total Surplus is our welfare scorecard, and only the competitive equilibrium drives DWL to zero.
Fig 8.2

Transfer vs. Destruction: The DWL Decomposition

When output is held below the efficient level Q* = 8, the lost consumer surplus splits into two categorically different pieces. A gold rectangle is transferred from buyers to the seller (redistributed, not lost). A red triangle is destroyed: it leaves the economy entirely. Slide the restriction left from Q = 8 and watch the triangle outgrow the rectangle.

Quantity Q: 4.0
What happens under a monopoly? A monopolist restricts output below Q* = 8 to raise price above MC = $2. The gold rectangle is the transfer: consumers lose it, but the firm gains it, so society as a whole is not worse off, it is simply redistributed. The red triangle is the deadweight loss: this value is destroyed and no one receives it. As Q falls further below Q*, the triangle grows faster than the rectangle: the true social cost (DWL) outweighs the redistribution.
Key insight: DWL is not a transfer; it is value destroyed. No one in the economy receives it. Monopoly profit (the rectangle) is largely a redistribution; the triangle is the true social cost.
Section 2 · Policy Applications
Fig 8.3

Trade and Tariffs: Welfare Effects

Opening trade at a world price below the domestic equilibrium raises total surplus: consumers gain more than producers lose. A tariff reverses some of these gains. Each pair of panels compares two states side-by-side to isolate the effect of trade (Part A) and the added cost of tariffs (Part B). Model: D : P = 20−Q; S : P = 4+Q; domestic equilibrium P* = $12.

Part A: Gains from Trade
No Trade (Autarky), P* = $12
Open Trade at World Price PW
World price PW: $6.00
Part B: Tariff Costs
Free Trade, No Tariff
With Tariff t: Effective Price PW + t
Tariff t: $0.00
Set a tariff above $0 to see the welfare cost.
Key insight: Tariff revenue does NOT offset the DWL: the two red triangles are destroyed value, not a transfer. Free trade maximises national welfare because the consumer gain exceeds the producer loss.
Section 3 · Monopoly
Fig 8.4

Monopoly Two-Step Rule: Guided Diagram

The single most-tested monopoly skill. Step 1: set MR = MC to find Q*M. Step 2: read the price up to the demand curve, never off MR. Walk the steps with the buttons, then toggle the common mistake to see exactly how reading P from MR understates the price.

Max buyer WTP (a): 10.0
MC c: 2.0
Profit = area of the gold rectangle. Look at the shaded gold box on the diagram. Its width = Q*M (units sold). Its height = P*M − MC (the markup per unit, how much more you charge than it costs to produce). Profit = width × height = Q*M × (P*M − MC). That is it. Profit is literally the area of that rectangle.
Key insight: Read the price from demand, not from MR. Setting MR = MC only locates the quantity; the demand curve tells you what buyers will actually pay for that quantity.
Fig 8.5

Competition vs. Monopoly Welfare Comparator

Toggle between the competitive outcome and the monopoly outcome on the same diagram. Under competition the whole CS triangle survives and DWL = 0. Under monopoly the CS triangle shrinks: part becomes the gold profit rectangle (transferred), and part becomes the red DWL triangle (destroyed). Adjust MC to see how the deadweight loss scales with market power.

Demand max WTP (a): $10.00
MC: $2.00
Key insight: The gold rectangle is a redistribution from consumers to the firm. The red triangle is the true social cost of monopoly, and CSmono + PSmono + DWL always equals TScomp.
Section 4 · Price Discrimination
Fig 8.7

Perfect Price Discrimination: Anatomy

Cycle through three states on one diagram. Under competition the CS triangle is large and DWL = 0. Under uniform monopoly CS shrinks, a gold profit rectangle appears, and a red DWL triangle is destroyed. Under perfect price discrimination the demand curve is sliced into thin slivers each priced at its buyer’s exact WTP: CS collapses to zero, but DWL also vanishes: every gain becomes profit.

Demand max WTP (a): $10.00
Marginal Cost (MC): $2.00
Key insight: Perfect price discrimination is the only form of PD that eliminates deadweight loss. But it requires the seller to know every buyer’s WTP: allocatively efficient, yet perfectly inequitable.
Fig 8.8

Block Pricing: Approaching PPD from 2nd Degree

With a single uniform price (n = 1) the monopolist sells Q = 4 at $6 and leaves a full DWL triangle. Offering more price tiers lets the firm trace a step-function approximation of demand: each block is priced at the willingness to pay of its last unit, so every unit in the block is voluntarily bought. As the number of tiers rises, output expands toward Q* = 8, DWL shrinks (DWL = 32/(n+1)²), profit rises, and the outcome approaches perfect price discrimination.

Number of tiers n: 1
Key insight: More price tiers → less deadweight loss. This is exactly why firms invest in market segmentation and versioning.
Fig 8.9

Market Separation: 3rd Degree PD

A monopolist selling in two separable markets sets MR₁ = MR₂ = MC to maximise total profit. Each market has its own demand and therefore its own price: the market where demand is less elastic always pays more. Click “Find Optimum” to see the full derivation; use the curve pickers to explore different demand shapes.

Market 1 · Inelastic (business)
Market 2 · Elastic (leisure)
MC: $20
Market 1 demand intercept (a₁): 100
Market 2 demand intercept (a₂): 50
Key insight: The price-discrimination rule is MR₁ = MR₂ = MC. Charge more in the less elastic market (lower price sensitivity = steeper demand = higher markup). Lerner index L = (P−MC)/P = −1/ε; higher |ε| (more elastic) → smaller markup → lower price.
Fig 8.10

Natural Monopoly Regulation Dilemma

With a large fixed cost, average cost declines over the whole range, so one firm serves the market most cheaply. But how to price it? Unregulated, the firm sets P > AC and creates DWL. Forcing P = MC is efficient but bankrupts the firm (it needs a subsidy). Forcing P = AC is the second-best compromise: zero profit, smaller DWL. Click each regime to compare. Model: $P = 20 - Q$, $TC = 50 + 2Q$, so $MC = 2$ and $AC = 50/Q + 2$.

Regulator’s dilemma: P = MC is efficient but causes losses that demand a subsidy. P = AC is second-best: the firm breaks even but some deadweight loss remains. There is no free lunch.
Fig 8.11

Two-Tier Pricing & Cross-Subsidization

A monopolist serving two consumer groups charges a high price PA to high-value customers (earning a green profit relative to AC) and a low price PR to price-sensitive customers (incurring a red loss relative to AC). High-tier profits cross-subsidize low-tier losses, allowing the firm to reach more consumers. Net profit = green area − red area. Drag the sliders to explore when cross-subsidization works. Model: D: P = 20 − Q; TC = 50 + 2Q; AC = 50/Q + 2; MC = $2.

High-tier price PA: $9
Low-tier price PR: $2
Key insight: Cross-subsidization requires AC(QR) to sit between PR and PA. High-tier markup covers fixed costs and subsidises below-AC pricing for the low tier. When green > red, net profit > 0; when green = red, the firm exactly breaks even, identical to the AC-pricing solution in Fig 8.10. Use the button to find the minimum PA that achieves break-even for the current PR.
