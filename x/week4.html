Elasticity Meter: Point vs Arc

Point elasticity measures responsiveness at a single location using the formula ed = −b·P/Q. Arc (midpoint) elasticity measures responsiveness between two points using the midpoint formula. Revenue Rectangle: Price vs Quantity Effect

Total revenue equals the area of the rectangle P×Q. When price rises, the green strip shows revenue gained from existing buyers paying more; the red strip shows revenue lost from buyers who leave. Whichever strip is bigger decides the change. Pricing Strategy Simulator

Each industry has a real-world empirical elasticity estimate. In Fig 4.4 below, predict the change in quantity and in total revenue for a price change; after the reveal, switch industries and change the price change. The solid bar is the exact %ΔTR, computed from the actual new price and quantity (constant-elasticity demand, Qnew = Qold × (Pnew/Pold)ed). The dashed outline is the linear approximation %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP × (1 + ed), which is close for small price changes and drifts away for large ones, especially for elastic goods.

Surge Pricing Simulator

Surge pricing on a ride-hailing app. As the surge multiplier rises, rider demand falls, consumer surplus shrinks, and the app's revenue grows, up to a point. Riders priced out exit the market entirely. This illustrates the welfare trade-off between producer revenue and consumer surplus.

Market Demand: Horizontal Aggregation

The market demand curve is the horizontal sum of individual demand curves. The animation below shows Consumer 1 (Q = 30 − P, choke price $30) and Consumer 2 (Q = 24 − 2P, choke price $12). Starting from a high price and moving down, watch each consumer enter the market and see how the kink forms when Consumer 2 begins buying. In Fig 4.8, build the market curve yourself: drag its top end, its kink and its bottom end into place.

Market Demand Formula by Price Range
P > $30:Qm = 0  (neither consumer buys; choke price of Consumer 1 not yet reached)
$12 < P ≤ $30:Qm = 30 − P  (Consumer 1 only; Consumer 2's choke price $12 not yet reached)
P ≤ $12:Qm = (30 − P) + (24 − 2P) = 54 − 3P  (both consumers buy; sum of demand curves)
The choke price is the price at which a consumer's demand falls to zero. Consumer 1: choke price = $30 (sets 30 − P = 0). Consumer 2: choke price = $12 (sets 24 − 2P = 0).

Check yourself

See all 20 cards with answers
  1. Define the price elasticity of demand. What does the sign always tell you?The percentage change in quantity demanded for a one percent change in own price: \(e_d = \dfrac{\%\Delta Q_X}{\%\Delta P_x} = \dfrac{\Delta Q_X}{\Delta P_x}\cdot\dfrac{P_x}{Q_X}\) Always negative: price and quantity move in opposite directions (Law of Demand). We report \(|e_d|\) to compare magnitudes.
  2. When is demand elastic, and what does it mean in plain English?Demand is elastic when \(e_d < -1\), i.e. \(|e_d| > 1\). The percentage change in quantity exceeds the percentage change in price. Consumers are price-sensitive - a small price increase drives a large quantity drop. Example: branded coffee (many near-substitutes available).
  3. When is demand inelastic, and what does it mean in plain English?Demand is inelastic when \(-1 < e_d < 0\), i.e. \(|e_d| < 1\). The percentage change in quantity is smaller than the percentage change in price. Consumers are price-insensitive - a price increase causes only a small quantity drop. Example: insulin, petrol (no close substitutes, necessity).
  4. What is unit elastic demand, and why is it significant for revenue?Demand is unit elastic when \(e_d = -1\), i.e. \(|e_d| = 1\). A 1% price change causes exactly a 1% change in quantity in the opposite direction. Total revenue \(TR = P \times Q\) is maximized here on a linear demand curve. A small price change in either direction leaves TR unchanged.
  5. For a linear demand \(Q = a - bP\), write the point elasticity formula and explain why it varies along the curve.\[e_d = -b \cdot \frac{P}{Q} = -b \cdot \frac{P}{a - bP}\] The slope \(\Delta Q / \Delta P = -b\) is constant everywhere on a linear demand. But the ratio \(P/Q\) rises as price rises (P grows while Q shrinks). So \(|e_d|\) increases toward the top - demand becomes more elastic at higher prices.
  6. State the arc (midpoint) elasticity formula. Why is it preferred over a simple point formula for large changes?\[e_{arc} = \frac{(Q_2 - Q_1)/\overline{Q}}{(P_2 - P_1)/\overline{P}}\quad\text{where }\overline{Q}=\tfrac{Q_1+Q_2}{2},\ \overline{P}=\tfrac{P_1+P_2}{2}\] The midpoint formula uses averages as the base, so it is symmetric: going from A→B gives the same number as B→A. A simple percentage-change formula gives different answers depending on direction.
  7. Elastic demand: what happens to total revenue when price rises? Show algebraically.TR falls when demand is elastic and price rises. \(TR = P \times Q\). When \(|e_d| > 1\): \[|\%\Delta Q| > |\%\Delta P| \implies \text{quantity effect dominates}\] Revenue lost from fewer units sold outweighs revenue gained from the higher price. \(P\uparrow \Rightarrow Q\downarrow\downarrow \Rightarrow TR\downarrow\) Conclusion: to raise TR with elastic demand, cut price.
  8. Inelastic demand: what happens to total revenue when price rises? Show algebraically.TR rises when demand is inelastic and price rises. \(TR = P \times Q\). When \(|e_d| < 1\): \[|\%\Delta Q| < |\%\Delta P| \implies \text{price effect dominates}\] Revenue gained from the higher price exceeds the revenue lost from fewer units sold. \(P\uparrow \Rightarrow Q\downarrow \Rightarrow TR\uparrow\) Conclusion: to raise TR with inelastic demand, raise price.
  9. Define income elasticity of demand. How does the sign differ for normal, luxury, and inferior goods?\[e_d^I = \frac{\%\Delta Q_X}{\%\Delta I}\] Normal good: \(e_d^I > 0\) - demand rises with income. Luxury good: \(e_d^I > 1\) - demand rises proportionally more than income. Inferior good: \(e_d^I < 0\) - demand falls as income rises. Cobb-Douglas demand always yields \(e_d^I = 1\) (normal, but not luxury).
  10. Define cross-price elasticity of demand. How does the sign classify goods as substitutes or complements?\[e_d^{CP} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_X}{\%\Delta P_y}\] Substitutes: \(e_d^{CP} > 0\). When \(P_y\) rises, consumers switch to X. Example: Coke and Pepsi. Complements: \(e_d^{CP} < 0\). When \(P_y\) rises, less Y is bought, and less X too. Example: coffee and creamer.
  11. True or False: Elasticity is constant along a linear demand curve.False. Along \(Q = a - bP\), slope \(\Delta Q/\Delta P = -b\) is constant, but elasticity is \(e_d = -b \cdot P/Q\). Since \(P/Q\) changes as we move along the curve, so does elasticity. Elasticity grows in magnitude (becomes more negative) at higher prices. The curve is elastic above its midpoint and inelastic below it.
  12. True or False: A steeper demand curve always implies lower elasticity at every price.False. Elasticity depends on both slope and the \(P/Q\) ratio. Two curves can have the same slope but very different elasticities at the same price if their intercepts differ. Rule: compare elasticities at the same \((P, Q)\) point - not just by looking at slopes.
  13. True or False: For \(Q = a - bP\), elasticity equals \(-1\) exactly at the midpoint of the demand curve.True. At the midpoint: \(P^* = a/(2b)\), \(Q^* = a/2\). \[e_d = -b \cdot \frac{a/(2b)}{a/2} = -b \cdot \frac{1}{b} = -1\] Above the midpoint: \(|e_d| > 1\) (elastic). Below: \(|e_d| < 1\) (inelastic). Total revenue is maximized at the midpoint.
  14. True or False: A firm can always raise revenue by cutting price.False. Cutting price raises revenue only when demand is elastic (\(|e_d| > 1\)) - the quantity gain dominates. If demand is inelastic (\(|e_d| < 1\)), cutting price lowers revenue because the quantity gain is proportionally smaller than the price cut. The correct rule: cut price when elastic; raise price when inelastic.
  15. Define consumer surplus. What area represents it on a standard demand diagram?Consumer surplus (CS) is the gap between a consumer's willingness to pay (the demand curve) and the price actually paid. \[CS = \text{Area of triangle above market price, below demand curve}\] For linear demand \(Q = a - bP\) at market price \(P^*\): \[CS = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot Q^*\cdot\left(\tfrac{a}{b} - P^*\right)\] where \(a/b\) is the choke price (where the demand curve meets the price axis).
  16. For \(Q = a - bP\) with market price \(P^*\), derive consumer surplus step by step.Step 1 - Choke price: set \(Q=0 \Rightarrow P_{\max} = a/b\). Step 2 - Quantity at \(P^*\): \(Q^* = a - bP^*\). Step 3 - CS = area of right triangle with base \(Q^*\) and height \((P_{\max} - P^*)\): \[CS = \tfrac{1}{2}(a - bP^*)\left(\tfrac{a}{b} - P^*\right)\] When price rises by \(\Delta P\), quantity falls by \(\Delta Q = b\Delta P\), and the CS loss = rectangle \((Q^* - \Delta Q)\Delta P\) (buyers who stay pay more) + small triangle \(\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta Q\cdot\Delta P\) (buyers who leave).
  17. At what price is total revenue maximized on a linear demand curve, and why?TR is maximized where \(e_d = -1\) - at the midpoint of the linear demand curve, \(P^* = a/(2b)\). For \(Q = a - bP\): \(TR = aP - bP^2\). Differentiating: \(dTR/dP = a - 2bP = 0 \Rightarrow P^* = a/(2b)\). Above \(P^*\): elastic region, TR rises as P falls. Below \(P^*\): inelastic region, TR falls as P falls. So the maximum is at the unit-elastic midpoint.
  18. Old Navy scenario: the company wants to raise revenue from clothing. What must they determine, and what is the likely recommendation?They need to estimate the price elasticity of demand for their clothing. If \(|e_d| > 1\) (elastic): cut price → TR rises. The quantity gain dominates. If \(|e_d| < 1\) (inelastic): raise price → TR rises. The price gain dominates. For fast-fashion with many close substitutes and flexible consumers: demand is likely elastic. Recommendation: run promotions and cut prices to drive volume, rather than raising prices.
  19. State two key determinants of price elasticity of demand and explain the direction of effect.1. Number of close substitutes - More substitutes → more elastic. If the price of X rises, consumers switch easily to alternatives. Narrow market definitions (Diet Pepsi) yield higher \(|e_d|\) than broad ones (soft drinks). 2. Time horizon - Longer run → more elastic. Consumers have more time to adjust behavior, find alternatives, or change capital (e.g. switch from petrol cars to electric). Short-run gasoline demand is inelastic; long-run is more elastic.
  20. Why does the market demand curve have a kink when consumers have different reservation prices?Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands at each price. As price falls below each consumer's reservation (choke) price, they enter the market - adding their demand slope to the total. At each entry price, the market demand curve becomes flatter (more responsive) because more consumers now react to price changes. The kink marks the price at which a new buyer segment joins. Example: if consumer A has choke price \$30 and B has \$15, the market curve kinks at \$15. Below \$15, both A and B respond, so quantity reacts more to each price change and the market curve is flatter than above \$15.
