Hidden Quality

The Market for Lemons: When Do Peaches Survive?

Buyers cannot tell a peach from a lemon, so they offer the average value \(\mathbb{E}[WTP] = 1{,}200 + 1{,}200p\), where \(p\) is the fraction of peaches. Peach sellers will only sell if that average clears their $2,000 reserve price. Above the knife-edge \(p^{*} = 2/3\) the market pools and both types trade; below it, the average is too low, peaches exit, and only lemons are left. Fig 12.1 below asks you to find the knife-edge and the price at half peaches; after the reveal, drag the point along the offer line to change the peach fraction.

Reading the graph

The rising blue line is what buyers will pay, the average value of a car drawn from the pool, \(\mathbb{E}[WTP] = 1{,}200 + 1{,}200p\); it climbs as the fraction of peaches \(p\) rises. The flat dashed line is the peach seller’s \(\$2{,}000\) reserve: they only sell if buyers offer at least that. Where blue crosses the reserve is the knife-edge \(p^{*} = 2/3\). The green region to its right is pooling (both types trade); the red region to its left is separating (peaches have exited).

The economics

Because buyers cannot tell a peach from a lemon, they price the average. When there are too few peaches, that average sits below what a peach is worth to its owner, so peach sellers walk away, which lowers the average further. The market is left trading only lemons even though buyers and peach sellers would both gain from a deal at the right price. This is adverse selection: hidden quality, not high cost, is what unravels the market, and every lost peach sale destroys a real \(\$400\) gain from trade.

Information can destroy a market. Adverse selection means a market that would function perfectly under full information can collapse: once the average price buyers will pay falls below the peach seller’s reserve, good cars exit, the pool worsens, and only lemons trade. The $400 gain from every peach sale is lost.

Credible Signals

Spence Signaling: The Separating Range

High-productivity workers earn \(y_H = \$100k\), low types \(y_L = \$40k\); education costs the high type \(c_H = s\) but the low type \(c_L = 3s\) (single crossing). An employer pays the high wage only above an education threshold \(s^{*}\). The threshold separates the types only when it is high enough that the low type will not mimic (\(s^{*} \ge 20\)) yet low enough that the high type still bothers (\(s^{*} \le 60\)). The green band is the whole separating range; \(s^{*} = 20\) is the least-wasteful one. In Fig 12.2 below, mark both edges of that range on the graph before the payoff lines appear; after the reveal, drag the threshold to see what each type nets.

Reading the graph

The horizontal dashed line is the low type’s outside option, the \(\$40\text{k}\) they earn without signaling. The two sloped lines are net payoffs from getting the education and being paid the high wage: the high type’s cost is \(c_H = s\) (gentle slope) and the low type’s is \(c_L = 3s\) (steep). The green band is the set of thresholds \(s^{*}\) that separate the types: high enough that the low type would rather stay out than mimic (\(s^{*} \ge 20\)), yet low enough that the high type still finds the signal worth it (\(s^{*} \le 60\)).

The economics

The whole mechanism rests on single crossing: the signal must be cheaper for the type it is meant to identify. Because education costs the low type three times as much, there is a range of diploma requirements only the high type will meet, and the employer can safely pay the high wage above it. Crucially, education adds no productivity here, so every dollar spent on it is pure social waste, a cost incurred solely to prove who you are. That is why the least demanding separating threshold (\(s^{*} = 20\)) is the efficient one: it separates the types at the smallest possible waste.

Single-crossing is the whole trick. A signal separates types only when it is cheaper for the high type, so the low type will not mimic it. Because education adds no productivity in this model, any positive signaling level is pure social waste, a cost paid solely to prove who you are.

  1. What is adverse selection? Why does the word 'adverse' apply?Adverse selection arises when one party has private information about a FIXED characteristic (quality, risk, type) that the other cannot observe BEFORE the transaction. Because buyers cannot tell high from low quality, they pay only the AVERAGE expected value. That price is too low for high-quality sellers, who exit, leaving a disproportionate share of low quality behind. The pool 'adversely' selects toward the worst types, hence the name. Akerlof's core insight: information asymmetry can destroy a market that would function perfectly under full information. The market for good used cars ('peaches') fails not because peaches lack value, but because buyers cannot distinguish them from lemons. Low quality imposes a negative externality on high-quality sellers through beliefs and prices.
  2. Explain market unraveling with more than two quality tiers. What is the deeper welfare harm?With several quality tiers, the failure CASCADES. In each round buyers update beliefs about the remaining pool, the expected value falls, and the next-highest tier finds the price unacceptable and exits. Three-type example (Low 50%, Medium 30%, High 20%): initial E[WTP] = $1,640 < High reserve ($3,000), so High exits. The pool re-weights, E[WTP] falls to $1,150 < Medium reserve, so Medium exits. Only lemons remain. The deeper harm: adverse selection is not merely 'more bad goods are traded.' In the extreme, GOOD GOODS STOP TRADING ENTIRELY, even though buyers and sellers would both gain. The welfare loss is the destruction of high-quality markets, not just a mismatch, mutually beneficial trades simply vanish.
  3. What is signaling, and what is the single-crossing condition that makes a signal credible?Signaling is an action taken by the INFORMED party to credibly communicate private information (e.g. a worker acquiring education to reveal ability). It works only under the single-crossing condition: the marginal cost of the signal must be LOWER for high-quality types than for low-quality types, \[\partial c_H/\partial s < \partial c_L/\partial s\] so only high types find it worth sending and low types cannot profitably mimic. Intuition: there must be a signal level high enough that the high type still gains (wage rise > cost) but the low type does not (cost > wage rise). If both types faced the same cost, the low type would copy the high type and the signal would convey nothing. Same logic underlies car warranties, brand reputation, professional certifications (CFA, CPA), and dividend policy.
  4. What is screening, and why must a screening menu distort the low-type contract?Screening is the mirror image of signaling: the UNINFORMED party designs a menu of contracts that induces each type to self-select. Classic case: insurance with different deductibles, low-risk buyers prefer high-deductible/low-premium, high-risk prefer low-deductible/high-premium. Effective menus require DISTORTIONS: the contract designed for the low-risk type must be made LESS attractive than its full-information optimum (partial coverage, higher deductibles) so that high-risk types will not want to mimic it. Consequence: the low-risk type bears the cost of the information asymmetry, receiving less-than-ideal coverage. This is the SECOND-BEST outcome, the best achievable when types cannot be observed directly. Contrast with signaling: in screening the uninformed party moves first (offers the menu); in signaling the informed party moves first (sends the signal).
  5. What is moral hazard, and why does a flat salary produce zero effort?Moral hazard arises when one party takes a hidden ACTION (effort) AFTER the contract is signed. An insured driver drives less carefully; a securitising bank under-screens loans. Why flat pay fails: with a fixed salary the agent's pay is identical whatever effort they choose, but effort is personally costly. So the agent rationally chooses the MINIMUM effort, they bear the cost of effort but capture none of its reward. The fix: tie pay to observable output (a positive performance weight beta on profit). This rewards effort and restores incentives, but it loads risk onto the agent because output also depends on luck. Timing diagnostic: moral hazard is EX POST (the hidden action is chosen after the deal), which distinguishes it from adverse selection (a hidden type that exists ex ante, before the deal).
  6. Lemons critical fraction. Lemon: WTP $1,200, reserve $1,000. Peach: WTP $2,400, reserve $2,000. Find E[WTP](p) and the critical peach fraction p*.Let p be the fraction of peaches. Buyers pay the expected value: \[E[WTP] = p(2{,}400) + (1-p)(1{,}200) = 1{,}200 + 1{,}200p\] Peaches trade only if this expected price covers the peach seller's reserve ($2,000): 1,200 + 1,200p >= 2,000, so 1,200p >= 800, and \[p^{*} = 2/3 \approx 0.667\] Interpretation: - p > 2/3: pooling equilibrium, E[WTP] exceeds $2,000, so peaches stay and both types trade. - p < 2/3: separating, E[WTP] falls below $2,000, peaches exit, only lemons trade. Check: at p = 0.75, E[WTP] = $2,100 > $2,000 (pool). At p = 0.5, E[WTP] = $1,800 < $2,000 (peaches exit).
  7. Spence separating range. y_H = $100,000, y_L = $40,000, c_H(s) = s, c_L(s) = 3s. Derive both incentive constraints and state the separating range.For a separating equilibrium, the high type signals (s* > 0) and the low type does not. Low type's constraint (must NOT want to mimic): staying at s = 0 beats faking s*: y_L - 0 >= y_H - 3s^{*}, so 3s^{*} >= 100,000 - 40,000 = 60,000, giving s^{*} >= 20 (in thousands). High type's constraint (must WANT to signal): signaling beats pretending to be low: y_H - s^{*} >= y_L - 0, so s^{*} <= 100,000 - 40,000 = 60,000, giving s^{*} <= 60 (in thousands). Separating range: \[20 \le s^{*} \le 60\] The minimum-cost (Riley) equilibrium is s^{*} = 20. Since education here adds no productivity, any s* > 0 is pure social waste, showing how asymmetric information causes over-investment in credentials.
  8. State the optimal performance weight in the principal-agent model and interpret its comparative statics.With a linear contract w = alpha + beta*(profit), the optimal performance weight is: \[\beta^{*} = \frac{1}{1 + r\sigma^{2}}\] where r is the agent's risk aversion and sigma^2 is the variance of the profit shock. Comparative statics: - As output noise sigma^2 rises, beta* FALLS: pay would reflect luck more than effort, so heavy performance weighting just imposes risk without sharpening incentives. - As risk aversion r rises, beta* FALLS: loading risk onto a more risk-averse agent is costlier, so insure more. This is the incentive-insurance trade-off: higher beta sharpens incentives but loads risk. Application: salespeople face steep commissions (their output closely tracks effort, low sigma^2), while research scientists get salary-heavy pay (noisy, long-horizon output, high sigma^2).
  9. Compare adverse selection and moral hazard across hidden variable, timing, distortion, tools, and the diagnostic test.Adverse selection vs Moral hazard: - Hidden variable: TYPE / quality (fixed) vs ACTION / effort (chosen). - Timing: EX ANTE, before contracting vs EX POST, after contracting. - Distortion: high quality crowded out of the market vs sub-optimal (too little) effort. - Tools: signaling, screening, certification vs performance pay, monitoring. - The test: a LIE DETECTOR (reveals hidden type) fixes adverse selection; a HIDDEN CAMERA (reveals hidden action) fixes moral hazard. The single most reliable diagnostic is TIMING. Ask: does the hidden thing exist before the deal (a pre-existing type -> adverse selection) or is it chosen after the deal (an action -> moral hazard)? The 2008 crisis had both: originators knew loan quality investors didn't (adverse selection) and stopped screening once they could offload risk (moral hazard).
  10. Insurance screening. Contract A (full $10,000 coverage, $5,000 premium) vs Contract B (partial $4,000 coverage, $400 premium). Accident chance 50% high-risk, 10% low-risk. Why must B be distorted?Expected net benefit = (accident prob x coverage) - premium. High-risk (50%): - A: 0.5(10,000) - 5,000 = $0. - B: 0.5(4,000) - 400 = $1,600. Low-risk (10%): - A: 0.1(10,000) - 5,000 = -$4,000. - B: 0.1(4,000) - 400 = $0. Judged by expected value alone, BOTH types prefer B, and shrinking B's coverage never changes that: any partial coverage c priced for low-risk buyers (0.1c) is worth 0.5c - 0.1c = 0.4c > 0 to a high-risk buyer, more than A's $0. So screening cannot work on expected value alone. What makes it work is RISK AVERSION. Full coverage (A) removes all risk, which a risk-averse buyer values above its expected payout. Partial coverage (B) leaves the buyer bearing the rest of a $10,000 loss, and that hurts the high-risk type most because they are the ones likely to have the accident. Cutting B's coverage makes it less attractive to high-risk buyers until they prefer the certainty of A, while low-risk buyers, who rarely claim, still take cheap B. That coverage cut is the DISTORTION: the low-risk type ends up under-insured relative to full information. It is the price of separating types when risk is unobservable, the second-best outcome.
  11. Common mistake: 'A signal works as long as it is costly.' What is missing?Cost alone is not enough. A credible signal must satisfy the SINGLE-CROSSING condition: it must be cheaper for the high type than the low type, \[\partial c_H/\partial s < \partial c_L/\partial s\] Why: separation requires a signal level that the high type is willing to send (benefit > cost for them) but the low type is not (cost > benefit for them). That gap only exists if the low type's marginal cost is steeper. If both types face the SAME cost of the signal, the low type can mimic the high type at identical expense, so the signal conveys no information and no separating equilibrium exists, no matter how expensive the signal is. So the test is not 'is it costly?' but 'is it DIFFERENTIALLY costly, cheaper for the type we want to identify?'
  12. Common mistake: 'Higher performance pay is always better' and 'signaling is always wasteful.' Correct both.Both overstate a half-truth. 'Higher performance pay is always better' is false. A larger beta sharpens incentives but loads risk onto a risk-averse agent. The optimum balances the two: \[\beta^{*} = \frac{1}{1 + r\sigma^{2}}\] When output is noisy (high sigma^2) or the agent is very risk-averse (high r), the optimal contract is FLATTER, more salary, less bonus, because extra performance weight would mostly transfer risk, not effort. 'Signaling is always socially wasteful' is also false. It is wasteful only if the signal adds NO productivity (pure Spence education that just sorts). If the education (or certification) ALSO raises real skill, the signal has dual value, sorting plus productivity, and may be socially efficient. Whether a signal wastes resources depends entirely on whether it does anything besides separate types.
