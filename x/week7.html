Profit Landscape (TR vs TC)

The firm earns profit wherever TR > TC: profit is the vertical gap between the two curves. In Fig 7.1 below, find the output where that gap is widest. The plum bar shows the gap at your guess, and a dashed line parallel to TR can be pulled down until it just touches TC. After the reveal, change the price or the fixed cost and move the marker to compare MR with MC at any quantity: q* is where the slopes are equal (MR = MC).

Key insight: The firm maximizes profit where MR = MC (the revenue from one more unit exactly equals its cost). To the left of q*, MR > MC (produce more). To the right, MC > MR (cut back).

Profit Rectangle Anatomy

The profit rectangle has height = P* − SAC(q*) and width = q*. The area = total profit. In Fig 7.2 below, draw the rectangle yourself; after the reveal, move the price P* along its axis and compare the common mistake of using SAVC as the bottom edge (it leaves out the fixed cost).

Shutdown Decision Zone Map

In Fig 7.3 below, find the two prices that divide the zones: below the shutdown price the firm should shut down, between the two it operates at a loss, at the break-even price it breaks even, and above it the firm earns a profit. After the reveal, move the price P* through the zones. The algebraic proof of the shutdown rule never changes. What changes is the price, and therefore which zone (and which decision) applies.

Algebraic proof: when to produce. Produce if the operating loss is smaller than the shutdown loss:
  1. Profit > −TFC → keep producing
  2. TR − TVC − TFC > −TFC
  3. TR − TVC > 0, so TR > TVC
  4. P·q > AVC·q
Conclusion: P > AVC. Produce whenever price covers average variable cost; shut down otherwise.

Supply Curve Emergence

Watch the price sweep upward from zero. Below the shutdown price (min(SAVC) = $4), the firm supplies nothing. Once price crosses $4, the SMC curve becomes the supply curve and each (q, P) point traces out in the right panel, building the supply curve dot by dot.

The core idea: how we get a supply curve out of a cost curve.
  1. Pick a price P.
  2. The firm maximizes profit by producing where P = SMC (the profit-maximizing rule).
  3. That intersection pins down a specific quantity q.
  4. Plot the point (q, P): that's one point on the firm's supply curve.
  5. Repeat for every price … and the dots you trace out land exactly on the SMC curve.
That's why the SMC curve (above the shutdown point) is the firm's supply curve: profit-maximization (P = SMC) directly maps each price to the quantity the firm will supply at that price.

Market Supply Builder

Market supply is the horizontal sum of all individual firm supply curves: at each price, add up the quantities each firm wants to supply. In Fig 7.8 below, predict where market supply starts and how much the market supplies at one price; after the reveal, change the number of firms and move the price to see exactly how the arithmetic works.

Key insight: At each price, add quantities across all firms; never add prices. If each of n identical firms supplies q units, market supply is Q = n × q. The market supply curve is flatter than any individual firm's supply curve (each $1 rise in price adds n times as many units) and shifts right as n grows. With identical firms its price elasticity at any given price is the same as one firm's: flatter is not the same as more elastic.

Long-Run Entry/Exit Machine

Each firm has the cost curves of Fig 7.8 (min SAC = $5.51 at q = 3.58), and market supply is n firms' SMC curves added together. Demand has just risen from D (Qd = 3,558.6 − 548.4P) to D′ (Qd = 4,086 − 548.4P), as in the animation. With 150 firms price is $6.40 and each firm earns $3.31 (green rectangle). New firms enter, market supply shifts right, the price falls, and the profit rectangle shrinks toward zero at long-run equilibrium (n ≈ 297, P* = $5.51 = minimum SAC, the flat LRS line). In Fig 7.9 below, predict that equilibrium and the number of firms; after the reveal, replay the entry or step the number of firms. An industry with too many firms (n = 450) runs the other way: price is $4.73, below SAC, the rectangle turns red (a loss of $2.69 per firm), and firms exit until price climbs back to the same zero-profit equilibrium at $5.51.

Zero economic profit is healthy: it means the firm earns exactly its opportunity cost. Owners are compensated fairly, just not earning excess returns.

  1. What is a price-taking (competitive) firm? What does it mean to be a 'price taker'?A price-taking firm is one that is too small relative to its market to influence the market price. It takes the price as given. Conditions: many buyers and sellers, homogeneous product, free entry and exit, perfect information. Consequence: the firm faces a horizontal (perfectly elastic) demand curve at the market price P*. It can sell any quantity at P*, but nothing at a higher price. MR = P* for a price taker: each additional unit sold adds exactly P* to revenue.
  2. State the profit-maximization rule. Derive it from the definition of profit.Profit: \(\pi = TR - TC\) Maximize: \(\frac{d\pi}{dq} = 0 \Rightarrow \frac{dTR}{dq} - \frac{dTC}{dq} = 0\) \[MR = MC\] For a price taker, MR = P, so the rule becomes P = MC. Second-order condition: MC must be rising at the optimum (so the firm is on the upward-sloping portion of its MC curve). If P > MC: produce more (extra revenue exceeds extra cost). If P < MC: produce less (extra cost exceeds extra revenue).
  3. Describe the profit rectangle on a per-unit diagram. What is its height? What is its width?On a diagram showing SMC and SAC curves with a horizontal price line P*: Optimal quantity q* is where P* = SMC (on the rising portion). Profit rectangle: - Width = q* (the output produced) - Height = P* - SAC(q*) (price minus average total cost) - Area = q* x (P* - SAC(q*)) = total profit If P* > SAC(q*): green (positive profit) rectangle above the SAC curve. If P* < SAC(q*): red (negative profit, a loss) rectangle below P*. If P* = SAC(q*): zero economic profit (break-even). Common mistake: using SAVC or SMC as the bottom of the rectangle instead of SAC.
  4. What is the shutdown rule? Why should a firm operate even when making a loss?Shutdown rule: Produce if P ≥ min(SAVC). Shut down if P < min(SAVC). Derivation: A firm shuts down only if it loses MORE by operating than by shutting down. - Shutdown loss = -TFC (still owe fixed costs) - Operating loss = TR - TVC - TFC Operate if: TR - TVC - TFC > -TFC → TR > TVC → P > AVC Intuition: if price covers variable costs, operating covers some fixed costs. Shutting down covers none. So operating is better even at a loss. Note: TFC is a sunk cost - it does not affect whether to produce, only P vs AVC does.
  5. Derive the break-even price and the shutdown price for a firm with TC(q) = q^2 + 4q + 9.Given: TC(q) = q^2 + 4q + 9. SMC = dTC/dq = 2q + 4. SAVC = TVC/q = (q^2 + 4q)/q = q + 4. SAC = TC/q = q + 4 + 9/q. Shutdown price = min(SAVC). d(SAVC)/dq = 1 > 0, so SAVC = q + 4 is always increasing and its minimum is at q → 0+: P_SD = $4. Break-even price = min(SAC). d(SAC)/dq = 1 - 9/q^2 = 0 → q* = 3. SAC(3) = 3 + 4 + 9/3 = 10. So P_BE = $10. Summary: P < $4: shut down. $4 ≤ P < $10: operate at a loss. P = $10: break even. P > $10: positive profit.
  6. What is the short-run supply curve of a competitive firm? How is it derived?The firm's short-run supply curve is the upward-sloping portion of the SMC curve at and above the shutdown price min(SAVC). Below min(SAVC): the firm supplies q = 0 (shuts down). At and above min(SAVC): the firm supplies the quantity where P = SMC. Derivation: For P ≥ P_SD, solve P = SMC for q. This gives q*(P), the supply function. For TC(q) = q^2 + 4q + 9: SMC = 2q + 4. Setting P = 2q + 4: q = (P-4)/2 for P ≥ 4. The supply curve slopes upward because SMC is increasing (law of diminishing returns).
  7. Why does MR = P for a competitive firm? Show the algebra.Total Revenue: TR = P x q (where P is fixed for a price taker). MR = dTR/dq = d(Pq)/dq = P. So MR = P always for a price taker. Geometrically: the demand curve facing the firm is horizontal at P*. Each unit sold adds exactly P* to revenue - there is no 'price effect' because the firm does not need to lower price to sell more. Contrast with a firm facing downward-sloping demand: each extra unit requires a price cut on ALL existing units, so MR < P.
  8. For a firm with downward-sloping demand P = a - bq, derive the MR formula and explain the decomposition.TR = P x q = (a - bq)q = aq - bq^2. MR = dTR/dq = a - 2bq. Observations: - Same intercept as demand (a), but twice the slope (-2b instead of -b). - MR < P for any q > 0 (since a - 2bq < a - bq). Decomposition: selling one more unit has two effects: 1. Gain: revenue from new unit = P_new (green rectangle, height P_new, width 1) 2. Loss: price cut on all existing units = q x (-dP) (red rectangle, height ΔP, width q) MR = P_new - q|dP/dq| = P + q(dP/dq) = (a-bq) + q(-b) = a - 2bq.
  9. Common mistake: 'The firm should shut down whenever it is making a loss.' Why is this wrong?This is wrong. A firm should compare two losses: 1. Operating loss = TR - TC = TR - TVC - TFC 2. Shutdown loss = -TFC (fixed costs still owed even if output = 0) The firm should operate if the operating loss is smaller than the shutdown loss: TR - TVC - TFC > -TFC → TR > TVC → P > AVC So: if P ≥ AVC, operate even at a loss (because revenue covers variable costs and contributes toward fixed costs). Only shut down if P < AVC (the firm cannot even cover variable costs). Key insight: TFC is sunk - it does not affect the shutdown decision. Only P vs AVC matters.
  10. What is zero economic profit in long-run equilibrium? Is this a bad outcome for the firm?In long-run equilibrium in a competitive market, free entry and exit drive economic profit to zero: \[P^* = \min(LRAC)\] This means: total revenue exactly covers all costs, including implicit costs (the opportunity cost of the owner's time and capital). Is this bad? No! Zero economic profit means the firm earns its opportunity cost - exactly what the owner's resources would earn in the best alternative use. Accounting profit is still positive (the owner is being paid their market wage and capital return). Key insight: 'zero profit' in economics means the firm is doing as well as it could anywhere else - a healthy, efficient outcome.
  11. Explain the long-run adjustment process when existing firms are earning positive economic profit.Positive economic profit attracts new firms to the market (free entry assumption). Step-by-step: 1. Firms earn positive profit: P* > min(SAC). 2. New firms enter: market supply curve shifts right. 3. Market price falls (as supply increases, price is bid down). 4. Each firm's profit rectangle shrinks. 5. Entry stops when P* = min(SAC) = min(LRAC), so profit = 0. Long-run equilibrium: n* firms, P* = min(LRAC), each firm produces at efficient scale. Symmetric process when losses occur: exit → supply shifts left → price rises → losses eliminated.
  12. Market supply: 100 firms each with supply q_i = (P-4)/2. Write the market supply function Q_S(P).Each firm: q_i = (P-4)/2 for P ≥ 4 (shutdown price = $4). Market supply: Q_S = 100 x q_i = 100 x (P-4)/2 = 50(P-4) for P ≥ 4. So: Q_S = 50P - 200 for P ≥ 4; Q_S = 0 for P < 4. Inversion: P = Q_S/50 + 4 (inverse supply). The market supply curve is flatter than an individual firm's supply curve: each $1 rise in P adds 50 units to market supply but only 0.5 to one firm's. With identical firms its price elasticity at any given price is the same as one firm's. As n rises: market supply shifts right → P* falls. This is the long-run entry mechanism.
  13. What is the three-panel causality diagram? Trace through a demand shock step by step.The three-panel diagram shows the market mechanism: Left panel: Typical firm (SMC, SAC curves) Center panel: The market (supply S and demand D curves) Right panel: Typical consumer (individual demand d) Shock: Price of a substitute rises. Causality chain: 1. Consumer panel: demand shifts from d to d' (substitute is more expensive, consumer wants more of this good). 2. Market panel: D shifts to D', new equilibrium at higher P* and Q*. 3. Firm panel: horizontal price line rises from P1 to P2; firm moves up SMC to produce more; profit rectangle appears. Key point: the market sets the price. Firms and consumers take it as given.
  14. How does a rise in an input price (e.g., wages) affect a firm's supply curve? Trace all effects.A wage increase shifts both SMC and SAVC upward. Effects on the firm: - SMC shifts up: for each output level, marginal cost is higher. - SAVC shifts up: minimum SAVC rises, so the shutdown price rises. - The supply curve (SMC above min SAVC) shifts left and up. Effects on the market: - Each firm's supply curve shifts left. - Market supply S shifts left. - Market price P* rises. - Market quantity Q* falls. - Firm output q* may rise or fall (P* up, but MC up too). Comparative statics: input price up → supply left → P* up, Q* down.
  15. A firm has TC(q) = (1/3)q^3 - 2q^2 + 7q + 5 and faces market price P = $8. Find q*, profit, and whether the firm should operate.SMC = dTC/dq = q^2 - 4q + 7. TVC = TC - TFC = (1/3)q^3 - 2q^2 + 7q, so SAVC = TVC/q = (1/3)q^2 - 2q + 7. Minimum at q = 3: SAVC(3) = 3 - 6 + 7 = 4. So P_SD = $4. SAC = (1/3)q^2 - 2q + 7 + 5/q. Min at q ≈ 3.58: SAC ≈ $5.51. Profit max: P = SMC → 8 = q^2 - 4q + 7 → q^2 - 4q - 1 = 0 → q = 2 + sqrt(5) ≈ 4.24. SAC(4.24) ≈ $5.69. Profit = (8 - 5.69) x 4.24 ≈ $9.79. Should the firm operate? Yes: P = $8 > $4 = P_SD.
  16. Distinguish between the shutdown price and the break-even price. What happens in each zone?Shutdown price = min(SAVC). If P < P_SD: firm shuts down (supply = 0). Break-even price = min(SAC). If P = P_BE: zero economic profit. Four zones: 1. P < min(SAVC): Shut down. Loss = -TFC. 2. min(SAVC) ≤ P < min(SAC): Operate at a loss. The loss is smaller than TFC (profit > -TFC), so operating is better than shutting down. 3. P = min(SAC): Break-even. Zero economic profit. 4. P > min(SAC): Positive economic profit. Note: min(SAVC) < min(SAC) always (because SAC = SAVC + AFC, and AFC > 0).
  17. Common mistake: 'The profit rectangle uses AVC as its bottom edge.' Why is this wrong?Wrong. The profit rectangle uses SAC (short-run average total cost) as its bottom edge, not SAVC (short-run average variable cost). Correct: Profit per unit = P* - SAC(q*). Total profit = q* x (P* - SAC(q*)). Why it matters: SAVC excludes fixed costs. Using SAVC as the bottom would give 'profit' that is actually just the contribution to covering fixed costs. Example: P* = $12, SAVC = $8, SAC = $11, q* = 5. - Correct profit: (12-11) x 5 = $5. - Wrong 'profit' using SAVC: (12-8) x 5 = $20 (this is total contribution margin, not profit). The contribution margin $20 goes to covering TFC = $15 and leaves $5 economic profit.
  18. What does the market supply curve look like in the short run vs. the long run? How do they differ?Short-run market supply: - Fixed number of firms n. - Upward sloping (sum of each firm's SMC above min SAVC). - Steeper and less elastic. Long-run market supply: - Free entry and exit drive profit to zero. - In perfect competition with identical firms and constant input prices: LRMS is horizontal at P* = min(LRAC). - Perfectly elastic (flat line). Why horizontal in the long run? Any P above min(LRAC) attracts entry → supply rises → P falls back. Any P below min(LRAC) causes exit → supply falls → P rises back. Long-run equilibrium is always at P* = min(LRAC). With upward-sloping input supply: LRMS slopes up (as industry grows, input prices rise).
  19. What is market supply? How is it constructed from individual firm supply curves?Market supply is the horizontal sum of all individual firm supply curves. Construction: At each price P, add the quantities supplied by every firm: \[Q_S(P) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i(P)\] Never add prices - always add quantities at a given price. Example: Firm A supplies q_A = P - 2; Firm B supplies q_B = 2P - 4 (for P ≥ 2). Market supply: Q_S = q_A + q_B = 3P - 6 for P ≥ 2. As n rises: market supply shifts right; market price falls; individual firms produce the same (their supply curve hasn't changed) but there are more of them.
  20. True or False: 'Economic profit > 0 means the firm's owner is getting rich.' Is this true?Not exactly. Here is the precise interpretation: Economic profit = Accounting profit - Implicit costs (opportunity costs of owner-supplied inputs). Economic profit > 0: the firm earns MORE than the owner's next-best alternative. The owner is genuinely better off in this business. Economic profit = 0: the firm earns EXACTLY the opportunity cost. The owner earns a fair return - not getting 'rich' in an economic sense, but not losing out either. This is the long-run equilibrium. Accounting profit is still positive at zero economic profit (the owner is being paid their market wage and return on capital). Bottom line: zero economic profit is healthy and efficient - it just means there are no excess returns attracting further entry.
