The Externality Wedge

Negative Externality & the Pigouvian Tax

Demand is \(P = 100 - Q\) and private marginal cost is \(PMC = 10 + Q\). Each unit also dumps a marginal external cost \(MEC\) on third parties, so social marginal cost is \(SMC = PMC + MEC\). The market produces where \(PMC = MB\) (too much); the optimum is where \(SMC = MB\). The red triangle is the deadweight loss. The Pigouvian tax \(t^{*} = MEC\) lifts private cost onto the social-cost curve and erases it. In Fig 11.1 below, find the market, the optimum and the tax on the graph yourself.

Reading the graph

The blue line is marginal benefit (the demand curve). The green line is private marginal cost, what the firm itself pays. The red line sits above it by the marginal external cost \(MEC\), the harm each unit dumps on third parties, so it is the true social marginal cost. The market settles where blue meets green (private cost = benefit) at \(Q_E = 45\). The efficient point is where blue meets red, at \(Q^{*}\). Every unit between \(Q^{*}\) and \(Q_E\) is worth less to buyers than it truly costs society, and those units stack up into the red deadweight-loss triangle.

The economics

Left alone, the polluter ignores \(MEC\) because it never pays it, so it overproduces. A Pigouvian tax equal to \(MEC\) at the optimum makes the firm feel that external cost, sliding its private-cost line up onto the social-cost line, so the market now chooses \(Q^{*}\) on its own. Notice the efficient amount of pollution is not zero: society tolerates the activity right up to the point where marginal benefit finally equals full social cost.

Internalize, don’t ban. The Pigouvian tax \(t^{*}=MEC(Q^{*})\) lifts private cost onto the social-cost curve, moving output to the efficient \(Q^{*}\) and erasing the deadweight loss. Note it does not stop the activity: some pollution remains, because the efficient level is where marginal benefit equals full social cost, not zero.

Least-Cost Abatement

Cap-and-Trade: Equalizing Marginal Abatement Cost

Two firms must together abate a total amount of pollution. Firm A cleans up cheaply (\(MAC_A = 2e_A\)); Firm B is expensive (\(MAC_B = 8e_B\)). A permit market makes every firm abate up to the same permit price, so the low-cost firm does more of the work. The result meets the target at least cost, far below forcing both firms to abate equally. In Fig 11.2 below, find the cheapest split yourself; after the reveal, change the total cap to see the permit price and the saving move.

Reading the graph

Each firm’s marginal abatement cost rises the more it cleans up. Firm A’s line (\(MAC_A = 2e_A\)) is flatter, cleaning is cheap for A, while Firm B’s (\(MAC_B = 8e_B\)) is steep. A permit market sets a single price, and every firm keeps abating until its own \(MAC\) reaches that price, which is the height where the two curves line up. Because A is the cheaper cleaner, A ends up doing most of the abating and B does little.

The economics

Total cost is minimized only when the last dollar spent on abatement buys the same reduction at every firm, that is, when their marginal abatement costs are equalized, which is exactly what one common permit price delivers. Forcing both firms to abate the same amount would leave A’s cheap capacity idle while B strains at high cost. Letting them trade reallocates the effort to whoever can do it cheapest, hitting the identical environmental target for far less money, here about a third less.

Let the cheap firm clean up. Equalizing marginal abatement cost across firms hits the pollution target at least total cost. Forcing every firm to abate the same amount wastes the low-cost firm’s advantage, here trading lets Firm A do most of the work and cuts total cost by 36%.

When the Spillover Is Positive

Positive Externality & the Pigouvian Subsidy

Some goods (vaccination, education, a new user joining a network) confer a marginal external benefit \(MEB\) on third parties, so social marginal benefit \(MSB = MB + MEB\) lies above private demand. The market equates \(MB = MC\) and under-produces; the optimum is where \(MSB = MC\). The red triangle is the deadweight loss from too little activity, and a Pigouvian subsidy \(s^{*}=MEB\) closes it. In Fig 11.3 below, draw the social benefit curve and pick the efficient quantity; after the reveal, change the external benefit and try any subsidy.

Reading the graph

Private demand (blue) understates the good’s true value, because each unit also confers a marginal external benefit \(MEB\) on other people. Adding that on top gives the higher social-benefit line \(MSB = MB + MEB\). The market trades where blue meets marginal cost and under-produces; the efficient quantity is further right, where the social-benefit line meets cost. The gap between them is a deadweight loss from too little activity, the red triangle.

The economics

A positive externality is the mirror image of pollution, so the fix is a subsidy rather than a tax. A subsidy of \(s^{*} = MEB\) pays the producer or consumer for the benefit they create for others, lifting private demand up onto the social-benefit line and raising output to the efficient level. This is the standard justification for public money going into vaccination, schooling, and research and development, activities whose benefits spill far beyond the person who pays for them.

Subsidize the spillover. A positive externality is the mirror image of a negative one: the market does too little, not too much, so the fix is a subsidy, not a tax. Setting \(s^{*}=MEB(Q^{*})\) pays for the benefit conferred on others and moves output up to the efficient level, which is why governments subsidize vaccines, schooling, and R&D.

Public Goods & Bargaining

Public Goods & the Free-Rider Problem

A public good (a neighborhood streetlight, a shared security guard) is worth providing when the group’s total willingness to pay exceeds its cost, even if no single person values it enough to fund it alone. But because no one can be excluded, each person waits for someone else to pay. Fig 11.4 below asks up to what cost the good is worth providing, and up to what cost one member buys it alone; after the reveal, change the group size and how much each member values the good against the \(\$300\) cost to see when provision is socially efficient, yet still fails because everyone free-rides.

Reading the graph

The left bar is the group’s total value (value per person × group size \(N\)); the dashed line across both bars is the good’s cost (\(\$300\) to start; drag it in Explore). Providing the good is socially efficient whenever the total-value bar clears the cost line. The right bar shows each individual’s own value: when it sits below the cost line, no single person values the good enough to pay for it alone.

The economics

Because a public good is non-excludable, everyone enjoys it whether or not they chip in, so each person’s best move is to wait for someone else to pay, to free-ride. The result is that a good clearly worth providing for the group as a whole can go unprovided, because no individual has the private incentive to fund it. The failure comes from non-excludability, not from the good being expensive: even when everyone would gain, the market under-provides. Real-world fixes coordinate the contributions (an all-or-nothing crowdfunding threshold) or simply finance the good through taxes.

Efficient, yet unprovided. Whenever total value exceeds the cost, providing the good is efficient, but each individual’s dominant move is to free-ride and hope the others pay. Non-excludability, not the cost, is what makes the market fail: the good is under-provided even though everyone would gain. Fixes coordinate contributions (an all-or-nothing crowdfunding threshold) or fund it through taxes.

The Coase Theorem: Bargaining to Efficiency

A factory’s marginal benefit from polluting falls as it pollutes more (\(MB = 100 - 2S\)); the marginal damage to a neighbor rises (\(MD = 2S\)). The efficient level is where they cross, \(S^{*}=25\). Coase’s insight: if property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, the two parties reach \(S^{*}\) regardless of who holds the right, only the split of the gains changes. In Fig 11.5 below, predict where bargaining ends from each starting right; after the reveal, pick who owns the right and move the pollution level one deal at a time to see the bargaining range close on \(S^{*}\).

Reading the graph

The downward line is the factory’s marginal benefit from polluting (\(MB = 100 - 2S\)), it falls because the easy, high-value pollution comes first. The upward line is the neighbor’s marginal damage (\(MD = 2S\)), which rises as pollution mounts. They cross at the efficient level \(S^{*} = 25\), where one more unit’s benefit to the factory just equals its harm to the neighbor. The two starting markers show the extremes: the polluter’s right (pollution starts high) and the neighbor’s right (pollution starts at zero).

The economics

Coase’s insight is that if the right is clearly assigned and bargaining is cheap, the two parties trade their way to \(S^{*}\) no matter who holds it, because whenever \(MB \neq MD\) there is a deal that makes both better off. Only the direction of payment changes: with the polluter’s right the neighbor pays it to cut back; with the neighbor’s right the polluter pays for permission to emit some. Efficiency is identical, only the distribution of the gains differs. The theorem breaks down when many parties are involved or bargaining is costly, which is exactly when we fall back on taxes and regulation.

Rights decide who pays, not the outcome. Start from the polluter’s right (high pollution) and the neighbor pays it to cut back; start from the neighbor’s right (zero pollution) and the polluter pays to be allowed some, either way they meet at \(S^{*}=25\). Efficiency does not depend on the allocation of rights, only on the ability to bargain, which is exactly why Coase fails when transaction costs are high or parties are many.

  1. Define the four-quadrant goods taxonomy using rivalry and excludability. Give an example of each.Goods are classified by two properties: rival (does one person's use reduce availability?) and excludable (can non-payers be shut out?). - Private (rival, excludable): food, clothing, airline seats. The market works, the seller can price and exclude. - Club (non-rival, excludable): Netflix, toll roads, gyms. A firm can charge an access fee even though MC is near zero. - Common-pool (rival, non-excludable): fisheries, aquifers, congested roads. Tragedy of the commons, over-exploitation. - Pure public (non-rival, non-excludable): national defense, broadcast radio, public health. Free-rider problem, under-provision. Key point: NON-EXCLUDABILITY is what makes the market fail. Non-rivalry only removes the efficiency reason to charge.
  2. What is a pure public good, and why does the free-rider problem arise?A pure public good is non-excludable (once provided, no one can be prevented from consuming it) AND non-rival (one person's use does not reduce availability, so the marginal cost of serving an extra consumer is zero). National defense and broadcast radio are classic examples. Free-rider problem: because non-payers cannot be excluded, each person waits for someone else to pay and then enjoys the benefit for free. In the extreme, the good is never provided even though everyone would benefit, the public-goods version of the prisoner's dilemma. Common trap: non-rival does NOT mean free to produce. Non-rivalry sets the efficient MARGINAL charge to zero, but the fixed cost (defense still costs hundreds of billions) must still be covered, usually through taxation.
  3. When is a public good worth providing, and how do partial contributions change the game?Provision is socially desirable when total willingness to pay exceeds cost: \[r_A + r_B > c\] even if no single person's WTP covers the cost alone. In the binary 'pay the full cost or not' game, Free-ride strictly dominates Contribute, so the unique Nash equilibrium is (Free-ride, Free-ride) and the good is not provided, a prisoner's dilemma. Allowing PARTIAL contributions (each pays a share) transforms it into a COORDINATION game with two Nash equilibria: the efficient one (both contribute their share) and the zero-provision one. The question shifts from 'will it be provided?' to 'can we coordinate on the good equilibrium?' Kickstarter implements this: backers are only charged if the target is met, so contributing becomes weakly dominant and the all-or-nothing threshold coordinates expectations on the efficient outcome.
  4. Define a negative and a positive externality. Which way does each distort the market quantity?An externality is a cost or benefit imposed on a third party, not reflected in market prices. Negative externality (factory pollution): social marginal cost exceeds private, \[SMC = PMC + MEC > PMC\] The market equates PMC = MB and OVERPRODUCES: Q^E > Q^*. Positive externality (vaccination, education, network goods): social marginal benefit exceeds private, \[SMB = MB + MEB > MB\] The market UNDERPRODUCES: Q^E < Q^*. Mechanism: the decision-maker weighs only their own (private) costs and benefits, ignoring the spillover. A negative externality means they ignore harm they cause, so they do too much; a positive externality means they ignore benefit they confer, so they do too little.
  5. State the Coase theorem, its three conditions, and when it breaks down.The Coase theorem: if property rights are well-defined, transaction costs are zero, and parties can bargain freely, the market reaches an efficient allocation REGARDLESS of who holds the right. Only the distribution of the gains changes, not the efficient pollution level. Three conditions: 1. Well-defined property rights. 2. Zero (or low) transaction costs. 3. Free bargaining. Breakdowns (common in practice): - High transaction costs when millions are affected. - Hold-up after sunk investments. - Information asymmetry about true costs and damages. - Free-riding among many victims. A single factory and a single downstream fishery with clear rights will bargain to efficiency on their own. But global carbon (hundreds of millions affected across countries) fails all conditions, so government intervention (a tax or cap) is the only path to the optimum.
  6. State the Pigouvian tax rule. Why is it evaluated at Q* and not at Q^E?A Pigouvian tax sets a per-unit tax equal to the marginal external cost AT THE SOCIAL OPTIMUM: \[t^{*} = MEC(Q^{*})\] The tax shifts the effective supply curve from PMC up to PMC + t* = SMC, moving the equilibrium to Q* and eliminating the deadweight loss. Why Q* and not Q^E: the tax should equal the external cost at the DESTINATION quantity you want to reach, not at the (larger) uncorrected market quantity. When MEC is constant, MEC(Q*) = MEC(Q^E) and it doesn't matter; but when MEC depends on Q, you must first solve for Q*, then evaluate MEC there. Taxing at MEC(Q^E) would over-tax and push output below the optimum. Note: the tax does not stop pollution entirely, it moves output to the efficient level, where some pollution remains because its marginal benefit still exceeds its full social cost.
  7. How does cap-and-trade minimize the cost of hitting a pollution target?The regulator sets a total pollution cap, issues permits equal to it, and lets firms buy and sell. At equilibrium every firm equates its marginal abatement cost to the permit price: \[MAC_A(e_A^{*}) = MAC_B(e_B^{*}) = \lambda^{*}\] Firms with LOW abatement costs clean up more and sell permits; firms with HIGH costs buy them. Trading reallocates abatement to whoever can do it cheapest, so the environmental target is met at MINIMUM total cost. Why equalizing MAC is optimal: if two firms had different marginal abatement costs, you could shift a unit of cleanup from the high-cost firm to the low-cost firm and cut total cost with the same total abatement. Only when all MACs are equal is no such improvement possible. This is exactly why equal regulation (forcing every firm to abate the same amount) is wasteful: it ignores cost differences.
  8. Externality benchmark. P = 100 - Q, PMC = 10 + Q, MEC = 20. Find Q^E, Q*, and the deadweight loss.Market equilibrium (PMC = MB): 10 + Q = 100 - Q, so 2Q = 90, Q^E = 45, P^E = $55. Social optimum (SMC = MB), with SMC = PMC + MEC = 30 + Q: 30 + Q = 100 - Q, so 2Q = 70, Q^* = 35, P^* = $65. Deadweight loss (triangle between SMC and MB over the overproduced units): \[DWL = \tfrac12(Q^E - Q^*)[SMC(Q^E) - MB(Q^E)]\] At Q^E = 45: SMC = 30 + 45 = 75, MB = 100 - 45 = 55, gap = 20. DWL = (1/2)(45 - 35)(20) = (1/2)(10)(20) = $100. The market overproduces 10 units, each with social cost exceeding its benefit, destroying $100 of surplus.
  9. Pigouvian tax with varying MEC. P = 200 - 3Q, PMC = 20 + Q, MEC = 2Q. Find Q*, the correct tax, and explain why MEC(Q^E) would over-tax.Social optimum: SMC = PMC + MEC = 20 + Q + 2Q = 20 + 3Q. Set = MB: 20 + 3Q = 200 - 3Q, so 6Q = 180, Q^* = 30. Correct tax: \[t^{*} = MEC(Q^{*}) = 2(30) = \$60\] Why MEC(Q^E) over-taxes: the uncorrected market quantity is larger. PMC = MB: 20 + Q = 200 - 3Q, so 4Q = 180, Q^E = 45. Then MEC(Q^E) = 2(45) = $90 > $60. Taxing at $90 would push output BELOW the optimum (over-correction), because $90 is the external cost at a quantity we are not trying to reach. The tax must equal marginal external cost at the destination Q*, not at the starting point Q^E.
  10. Cap-and-trade cost saving. MAC_A = 2e_A, MAC_B = 8e_B, total abatement = 100. Find the efficient allocation, permit price, and cost vs equal regulation.Equalize marginal abatement costs: 2e_A = 8e_B, so e_A = 4e_B. With e_A + e_B = 100: 4e_B + e_B = 100, e_B^{*} = 20, e_A^{*} = 80. Permit price: lambda^{*} = 2(80) = 8(20) = $160. Total cost (area under each MAC) = (1/2)(2)(80^2) + (1/2)(8)(20^2) = 6,400 + 1,600 = $8,000. Equal regulation (e_A = e_B = 50): (1/2)(2)(50^2) + (1/2)(8)(50^2) = 2,500 + 10,000 = $12,500. Saving = $4,500, about 36%. The low-cost firm A does most of the cleanup (80 vs 20). Equal regulation wastes firm A's cost advantage by forcing the high-cost firm B to over-abate.
  11. Free-rider game. Three neighbors can jointly fund a $300/month guard; each values it at $120/month, deciding alone. Is it optimal? Will anyone pay? What surplus is missed?Socially optimal? Total WTP = 3 x $120 = $360 > $300 cost. Net social surplus = $60. Yes, it should be provided. Will anyone pay (deciding independently, full-cost contribution)? No. Each neighbor reasons: 'if the other two pay, I benefit for free; if they don't, my $300 alone is wasted.' Free-riding is the dominant choice, so the guard is not hired. Missed net surplus = $60. This is the free-rider problem: individually rational free-riding produces a collectively irrational outcome (no guard, despite $360 of value for $300 of cost). Fixing it needs a mechanism, mandatory cost-sharing, a coordinating threshold (Kickstarter-style), or government provision funded by taxes.
  12. Common mistake: computing the externality deadweight loss from the MEC curve alone. What is the correct measurement?Wrong. The DWL triangle is NOT the area under MEC by itself. The correct triangle uses the VERTICAL GAP between the social marginal cost (SMC) and the demand curve (MB), measured at the market quantity Q^E, over the overproduced range from Q* to Q^E: \[DWL = \tfrac12(Q^E - Q^*)[SMC(Q^E) - MB(Q^E)]\] Why: the loss on each excess unit is how much its social cost exceeds the benefit consumers get, i.e. SMC minus MB, not the external cost MEC in isolation. At Q^E the private market has set PMC = MB, so the SMC-minus-MB gap equals the MEC there, but you must anchor the triangle on the gap between the social-cost and demand curves, then shrink it to zero at Q*. Always draw SMC and MB and read the wedge between them.
