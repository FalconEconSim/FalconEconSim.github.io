Indifference Curves

An indifference curve (IC) shows all bundles \((X, Y)\) that give a consumer exactly the same utility. Geometrically, ICs are level curves of the utility function \(U(X,Y)\): just as a topographic map shows lines of equal elevation, each IC traces a line of equal satisfaction. The shape of the IC encodes the entire preference structure.

Key Idea
Well-behaved ICs are downward-sloping (both goods have positive marginal utility), convex toward the origin (diminishing marginal rate of substitution), and never intersect (a direct consequence of transitive preferences).
Economic Intuition: Preference Types & the Shape of ICs The shape of the IC encodes everything about preferences. For Cobb-Douglas, a controls the spending share on X: a = 0.7 means 70% of income always goes to X regardless of prices. Perfect substitutes have a constant MRS everywhere: for \(U = aX + bY\) the MRS is a/b. Fig 2.1 below uses the equal-value case a = b = 1, where the consumer swaps one X for one Y; Fig 2.10 lets you change the marginal utility of X. Perfect complements have ICs that are L-shaped: consuming more of one good without the other adds zero utility (like left and right shoes). Moving to a higher IC always means higher utility, so ICs further from the origin are always preferred.
Economic Intuition: Why IC Shape Drives Market Strategy The shape of indifference curves has direct business implications. When two goods are perfect substitutes (straight-line ICs), consumers buy only the cheapest option, which is exactly the threat private-label brands pose to name brands. Procter & Gamble invests in advertising to shift consumers away from linear ICs toward convex ones, creating a preference for the brand itself. Perfect complements (L-shaped ICs) underlie the razor-and-blade business model: Gillette prices handles near cost and charges a premium on blades because both must be used in fixed proportions. Apple's iPhone/App Store ecosystem follows the same logic. Economic bads (upward-sloping ICs) are central to environmental policy: a longer commute is a bad, so workers require higher wages as compensation: the wage premium reflects the upward slope of their IC over (income, commute time).
Common Mistake
Indifference curves never intersect. Suppose two ICs crossed at bundle A. Then A lies on both curves, meaning the consumer is indifferent between bundles on either curve. But bundles on the outer IC represent strictly more utility, which contradicts the indifference. The formal culprit is transitivity of preferences.

The Budget Constraint

The budget constraint \(P_X X + P_Y Y = I\) defines the boundary of the affordable set. Its slope \(-P_X/P_Y\) is the relative price of \(X\) in terms of \(Y\): it tells you how many units of \(Y\) you must give up to get one more unit of \(X\).

Budget Constraint, slope-intercept form
\[Y = \frac{I}{P_Y} - \frac{P_X}{P_Y}\, X\]
Y-intercept: \(\,I / P_Y\) X-intercept: \(\,I / P_X\) Slope: \(\,-P_X / P_Y\)
Economic Intuition (LO 5): The Budget Constraint as an Opportunity Set The slope \(−P_X/P_Y\) is the relative price: how many units of \(Y\) you must give up to get one more unit of \(X\) at market prices. This is the market's trade-off. When income rises, both intercepts move out proportionally, and the slope is unchanged because the price ratio is unchanged. When \(P_X\) rises, only the \(X\)-intercept moves inward and the line pivots: \(Y\) becomes relatively cheaper, so the consumer's opportunity set shrinks on the \(X\)-axis.

Income & Price Changes

Income changes shift the budget line in parallel (slope unchanged). Price changes pivot it around the fixed intercept.

Economic Intuition (LO 6): Shifts vs. Pivots An income change is a parallel shift: it multiplies both intercepts by the same factor (1 + Δ%) so the slope \(-P_X/P_Y\) is unchanged. A price change pivots the line around the unchanged intercept: raising \(P_X\) rotates the line inward around the fixed Y-intercept (\(I/P_Y\) stays constant, \(I/P_X\) falls). The key test: did the slope change? If yes → price change. If no → income change.
Real-World Example: Fuel Tax vs. Income Tax A fuel tax pivots the budget line inward: only the fuel intercept moves and the slope changes. A lump-sum income tax shifts it inward in parallel: both intercepts move by the same proportion and the slope is unchanged. Both can raise the same revenue, but only the fuel tax changes relative prices and nudges consumption away from fuel.

The Consumer's Best Choice

The consumer's optimal bundle satisfies two conditions at once. First, the bundle must lie on the budget line (all income is spent). Second, the indifference curve through that bundle must be tangent to the budget line. At the tangency, the slope of the IC equals the slope of the budget line:

Two Conditions for the Best Affordable Bundle
Condition 1, Budget Exhausted
\[P_X X^* + P_Y Y^* = I\]
Every dollar is spent.
Condition 2, Tangency
\[\frac{P_X}{P_Y} = \frac{MU_X}{MU_Y} = MRS\]
Market trade-off = personal trade-off.
Economic Intuition (LO 7): Why Tangency is Optimal At any non-tangency point on the budget line, the IC slope (MRS) ≠ budget line slope (\(P_X/P_Y\)). If MRS > \(P_X/P_Y\), the consumer values X more than markets do, so they should buy more X. They keep adjusting until MRS = \(P_X/P_Y\). At the tangency, the personal willingness to trade (MRS) exactly matches the market's trade-off (price ratio). No further reallocation can improve utility. Watch the figure: the optimal IC is tangent to the budget line; the ICs above are unaffordable; the ICs below are sub-optimal.
Common Mistake
The tangency condition applies only to smooth, convex ICs. For perfect complements, use the kink condition \(X/a = Y/b\). For perfect substitutes, compare \(MU_X/P_X\) vs \(MU_Y/P_Y\) and buy only the better-value good.

Marginal Utility per Dollar

The tangency condition \(P_X/P_Y = MU_X/MU_Y\) can be rearranged to show the same optimality in a different form. Cross-multiplying gives the marginal utility per dollar condition:

Marginal Utility per Dollar Condition (rearranged tangency)
\[\frac{MU_X}{P_X} = \frac{MU_Y}{P_Y}\]
Utility per dollar must be equalized across all goods at the optimum
Key Idea
If \(MU_X/P_X> MU_Y/P_Y\), spending one less dollar on \(Y\) and one more on \(X\) raises utility. The consumer keeps reallocating until both ratios are equal. This equimarginal principle extends far beyond consumer theory: it governs optimal advertising budgets, hospital resource allocation, and government program spending.
Economic Intuition (LO 8): The Equimarginal Principle in Business The bang-for-the-buck condition \(MU_X/P_X = MU_Y/P_Y\) is the foundation of rational resource allocation wherever budgets are constrained. A coffee shop owner splitting $10,000 between espresso machine upgrades and barista training should keep spending on whichever gives more revenue per dollar, stopping only when the marginal return per dollar is equal across both investments, which is the condition satisfied by the optimum in the figure. Netflix applies the same logic across content categories: drama, comedy, documentary each get funding up to the point where the last dollar spent generates equal viewer-minutes per dollar. A hospital system allocating staff across departments faces the identical problem. The great insight of consumer theory is that this is not a special business rule: it is the universal condition for optimality whenever resources are scarce and alternatives exist.

MRS Intuition: Watch It Fall Along the Curve

The MRS is the slope of the indifference curve at any point. For a Cobb-Douglas consumer, MRS falls as you move right along the curve (diminishing MRS). Why Indifference Curves Cannot Cross

Indifference curves never intersect. If they did, the transitivity of preferences would be violated. In Fig 2.9 below, draw a second curve for the same consumer: if your drawing crosses the first one, the figure marks the crossing and spells out the contradiction.

The Proof
Suppose two indifference curves, IC1 and IC2, cross at bundle A. Pick bundle B on one curve and bundle C directly below it on the other. B shares a curve with A, so A ~ B; C shares the other curve with A, so A ~ C. By transitivity, B ~ C. But B has the same X as C and strictly more Y, so with monotone preferences B must be strictly preferred to C. Contradiction: two indifference curves can never cross.

See all 20 cards with answers
  1. What is a budget constraint, and what does it represent geometrically?All combinations of goods a consumer can afford given income \(I\) and prices \(P_x, P_y\). Geometrically: a straight line with x-intercept \(I/P_x\), y-intercept \(I/P_y\), and slope \(-P_x/P_y\). Points on or inside the line are feasible.
  2. What does the slope of the budget line equal, and what does it mean economically?Slope \(= -P_x/P_y\). The market's rate of exchange: how many units of Y you must give up to buy one more unit of X. This is the opportunity cost of X in terms of Y.
  3. What is an indifference curve, and what property guarantees they never cross?A curve showing all bundles yielding the same utility. They never cross because preferences are transitive and monotone. If two curves crossed, a single bundle would imply two different utility levels, a contradiction.
  4. Define the Marginal Rate of Substitution. How is it related to marginal utilities?MRS = the rate at which a consumer willingly trades Y for one more X, holding utility constant. \(\text{MRS} = MU_x / MU_y\). It is the ratio of marginal utilities, and it equals the absolute value of the slope of the indifference curve at any bundle; the slope of the IC itself is \(-\text{MRS}\).
  5. What is the consumer optimum? State it in both graphical and algebraic terms.The affordable bundle maximizing utility. Graphically: the highest indifference curve tangent to the budget line. Algebraically: \(\text{MRS} = P_x/P_y\) and \(P_x x + P_y y = I\) both hold simultaneously.
  6. What distinguishes a corner solution from an interior solution?An interior solution has \(x^*>0\) and \(y^*>0\) with tangency condition \(\text{MRS}=P_x/P_y\). A corner solution has \(x^*=0\) or \(y^*=0\). The consumer spends all income on one good (e.g. perfect substitutes when MRS does not equal \(P_x/P_y\) at any interior point).
  7. A steeper indifference curve implies a higher MRS.True. MRS equals the absolute slope of the indifference curve. Steeper = larger MRS. The consumer requires more Y to compensate for losing one unit of X, reflecting stronger preference for X at that bundle.
  8. If income doubles and both prices double, the budget set is unchanged.True. The budget set is \(P_x x + P_y y \leq I\). Multiplying \(I\), \(P_x\), and \(P_y\) by the same constant leaves intercepts \(I/P_x\) and \(I/P_y\) unchanged. Only relative prices and real income matter.
  9. Two indifference curves for the same consumer can intersect if the consumer is indifferent between the bundles.False. If they crossed, the intersection bundle would lie on both curves, implying two different utility levels simultaneously, violating transitive, consistent preferences.
  10. At the consumer optimum, the consumer has exhausted all gains from trade with the market.True. At \(\text{MRS}=P_x/P_y\), subjective valuation equals the market's exchange rate. No reallocation of spending can increase utility. All mutually beneficial trades have been made.
  11. For perfect complements, the MRS is defined everywhere along the indifference curve.False. Perfect complement curves are L-shaped. At the kink (vertex) MRS is undefined. Along vertical/horizontal segments the slope is 0 or infinity.
  12. A rise in \(P_x\) rotates the budget line inward along the x-axis only, leaving the y-intercept unchanged.True. The y-intercept \(I/P_y\) is unaffected by a change in \(P_x\). The x-intercept \(I/P_x\) falls as \(P_x\) rises. The line pivots inward around the y-intercept, becoming steeper.
  13. At the consumer optimum, MRS = ___\(\text{MRS} = P_x / P_y\) The tangency condition. Consumer's willingness to trade must equal the market's rate of exchange. If \(\text{MRS} > P_x/P_y\), the consumer values X more than the market and should buy more X.
  14. For \(U = X^\alpha Y^\beta\) (Cobb-Douglas), the optimal share of income spent on X is ___\(P_x x^* = \dfrac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta} \cdot I\) A fixed fraction \(\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\) of income is always spent on X, regardless of prices. Demand for X is independent of \(P_y\).
  15. The budget constraint can be written as ___ = I\(P_x \cdot x + P_y \cdot y = I\) Spending on X plus Y exhausts income. Points below the line leave income unspent, which is suboptimal under monotone preferences.
  16. \(\text{MRS} = MU_x/MU_y\). For \(U = xy\), compute MRS at bundle (2, 4).\(MU_x = y = 4,\quad MU_y = x = 2\) \(\text{MRS} = MU_x/MU_y = 4/2 = 2\) At (2, 4) the consumer is willing to give up 2 units of Y for 1 more X (\(\text{MRS}=2\)). If \(P_x/P_y < 2\), they should buy more X.
  17. For perfect substitutes \(U = aX + bY\), what is the optimum when \(P_x/P_y < a/b\)?Corner solution: \(x^* = I/P_x,\; y^* = 0\) X is cheap relative to how much the consumer values it. Every dollar buys more utility via X, so spend all income on X. \(\text{MRS} = a/b > P_x/P_y\) at every interior point.
  18. A bundle lies strictly below the budget line. Is it feasible? Is it optimal? Why or why not?Feasible: Yes, since \(P_x x + P_y y < I\). Optimal: No. Under monotone preferences there always exists a bundle on the budget line with more of at least one good. Leaving income unspent cannot be optimal.
  19. The budget line cuts through an indifference curve (not tangent). Can the consumer do better?Yes. When the line crosses the curve, part of the budget line lies above it, reaching a higher indifference curve. The consumer moves along the budget line to higher utility. Only at tangency is no improvement possible.
  20. How does the optimal bundle shift when \(P_x\) falls, for a Cobb-Douglas consumer? Which curve moves?Budget line rotates outward (y-intercept fixed; x-intercept rises). Indifference curve map is fixed. New optimum is on a higher curve. \(x^* = \alpha I/[(\alpha+\beta)P_x]\) rises; \(y^*\) unchanged.
