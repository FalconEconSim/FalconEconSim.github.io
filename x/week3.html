Two-Panel Demand Derivation

Every price of X pins down one optimal bundle in the utility maximization panel. Now let's change the price of X and see what changes. As the price falls, the budget line pivots outward, the consumer reaches a new indifference curve, and a new optimal point emerges. That bundle's X-coordinate is then plotted in the demand panel below it, building the demand curve one point at a time. Watch the film, then find one of those points yourself in Fig 3.1 below: pull the dashed indifference curve until it just touches the new budget line.

Slutsky Decomposition Explorer

Any price change can be split into a substitution effect (SE), moving along the original IC at new prices, and an income effect (IE), shifting between ICs due to changed real income. In Fig 3.2 below, build the compensated budget line yourself and find C; after the reveal, change the new price and watch the decomposition update live.

Remember: The compensated budget line has the new slope (-PX,new/PY) but provides just enough income to maintain the original utility UA. Point C is its tangency with the original IC. The SE always moves X opposite to the price: negative when PX rises, positive when it falls (as at the default here).

Income Effects & Demand Shifts

A change in income shifts the entire demand curve. For a normal good, rising income shifts demand rightward; for an inferior good, rising income shifts demand leftward. Predict both moves in Fig 3.3 below, then switch between the two types of good and watch the income expansion path trace out.

Cross-Price & Demand Function Explorer

Two types of price effects show up on a demand diagram, and they look completely different. Moving the own price (PX) traces a path along the existing demand curve: quantity adjusts, but the curve itself stays put. Changing the cross price (PY, the price of another good) shifts the entire curve: at every possible PX, demand for X is now higher or lower than before.

Why the difference? The demand curve for X already embeds a fixed PY. When PY changes, that embedded assumption breaks: you need a whole new curve. The direction of the shift reveals the relationship: if X and Y are substitutes (think Pepsi vs Coke), a higher PY makes X look relatively cheaper and shifts demand for X rightward. If they are complements (think coffee and milk), a higher PY makes the combination more expensive and shifts demand for X leftward.

Try this in Fig 3.5 below once you have answered: set the relationship to Substitutes, then hold PX fixed and raise PY. Watch the demand curve shift right, meaning at the same PX, consumers now want more X because Y just got more expensive. Then switch to Complements and repeat: the shift reverses.

Key distinction: Changing PX causes a movement along the demand curve (the current point slides). Changing PY or I shifts the entire curve.

Check yourself

20 flashcards on this week. They come back on a schedule: the ones you miss soon, the ones you know days later.

See all 20 cards with answers
  1. What is a consumer's demand function?A demand function expresses the quantity demanded of a good as a function of its own price, prices of other goods, and income: \(X^* = f(P_x, P_y, I)\) It is derived by solving the utility-maximization problem for every possible price-income combination.
  2. State the Law of Demand.The Law of Demand states that, holding income and other prices constant, the quantity demanded of a good falls when its own price rises: \(\frac{\partial X^*}{\partial P_x} < 0\) This holds for normal goods and most inferior goods. The exception is the Giffen good.
  3. How is a demand curve derived from the IC/budget constraint diagram?Vary \(P_x\) while holding \(I\) and \(P_y\) fixed. At each price: 1. The budget line pivots around the fixed Y-intercept \(I/P_y\). 2. Find the new optimal bundle \((X^*, Y^*)\). 3. Plot \((X^*, P_x)\) in a separate panel. Connecting these points traces the demand curve. This is the price-consumption curve projected onto the price-quantity plane.
  4. For Cobb-Douglas \(U=X^\alpha Y^\beta\), what is the demand for X?\[X^* = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\cdot\frac{I}{P_x}\] For \(\alpha + \beta = 1\): \(X^* = \alpha I / P_x\). The consumer always spends fraction \(\alpha\) of income on X, regardless of prices. This is the constant-budget-share property.
  5. What is a normal good?A normal good is one whose demand increases when income rises, holding prices fixed: \(\frac{\partial X^*}{\partial I} > 0\) Most goods are normal. Cobb-Douglas always yields normal goods. The income expansion path slopes upward for normal goods.
  6. What is an inferior good?An inferior good is one whose demand decreases when income rises: \(\frac{\partial X^*}{\partial I} < 0\) Classic examples: bus rides (as income rises, people switch to cars), instant noodles, generic staples. The income expansion path bends backward for inferior goods.
  7. What is the substitution effect (SE)?The substitution effect measures the change in quantity demanded due purely to the change in relative prices, holding utility constant (moving along the same IC). It is always negative for own-price changes: when \(P_x\) rises, SE causes less X to be consumed. \(\text{SE} = X_C - X_A < 0\) when \(P_x \uparrow\)
  8. What is the income effect (IE)?The income effect measures the change in quantity demanded due to the change in real purchasing power, holding relative prices constant (shifting between ICs). For normal goods, IE reinforces SE (both negative when \(P_x\) rises). For inferior goods, IE is positive (partially offsets SE). \(\text{IE} = X_B - X_C\)
  9. State the Slutsky decomposition.Any total price effect can be decomposed as: \[\underbrace{X_B - X_A}_{\text{Total Effect}} = \underbrace{(X_C - X_A)}_{\text{SE}} + \underbrace{(X_B - X_C)}_{\text{IE}}\] Where A = original bundle, C = compensated bundle (on original IC at new prices), B = new bundle. For Cobb-Douglas with \(\alpha = \beta = 0.5\): \(X_C = X_A\sqrt{P_{X,old}/P_{X,new}}\)
  10. True or False: For a normal good, when \(P_x\) rises, both SE and IE are negative.TRUE. When \(P_x\) rises: - SE < 0: Consumer substitutes away from the now-more-expensive X. - IE < 0: Rising \(P_x\) reduces real purchasing power; for a normal good, lower real income means less X demanded. Both effects reduce \(X^*\), so the demand curve must slope downward.
  11. True or False: For an inferior good, when \(P_x\) rises, SE and IE have opposite signs.TRUE. When \(P_x\) rises: - SE < 0 (always): Consumer substitutes away from X. - IE > 0: Lower real income leads to MORE X demanded (since X is inferior). The net effect depends on which dominates. Usually |SE| > |IE|, so demand still falls. If |IE| > |SE|, you get a Giffen good.
  12. What is a Giffen good?A Giffen good is an inferior good for which demand RISES when its price rises: \(\frac{\partial X^*}{\partial P_x} > 0\) This requires: 1. X must be a strongly inferior good (large negative IE). 2. X must occupy a large share of the budget. The income effect dominates the substitution effect in magnitude. Giffen goods are theoretically possible but extremely rare in practice.
  13. What is the income expansion path (IEP)?The income expansion path traces the locus of optimal bundles as income varies (with prices held fixed). - Normal goods: IEP slopes upward and to the right (ray from origin for Cobb-Douglas). - Inferior goods: IEP bends backward (demand for the inferior good eventually decreases as income rises). The IEP is the income analog of the price-consumption curve.
  14. What is the price-consumption curve (PCC)?The price-consumption curve traces the locus of optimal bundles as \(P_x\) varies (with income and \(P_y\) fixed). For Cobb-Douglas \(U=X^{0.5}Y^{0.5}\): - \(Y^* = I/(2P_y)\) is constant as \(P_x\) changes. - So the PCC is a horizontal line at \(Y^* = I/(2P_y)\). Projecting the PCC onto the \((X, P_x)\) plane gives the demand curve.
  15. What is the compensated (Hicksian) demand?Compensated demand \(X_C\) is the quantity demanded when the consumer is given just enough income to maintain original utility \(U^*\) at the new prices. For Cobb-Douglas with \(\alpha=0.5\): \[X_C = X_A \cdot \sqrt{\frac{P_{X,old}}{P_{X,new}}}\] The compensated budget line has the new price slope but is tangent to the original IC at point C.
  16. Difference: shift of demand curve vs. movement along it?Movement along the demand curve: caused by a change in the GOOD'S OWN PRICE \(P_x\). The consumer moves from one point to another on the same demand curve. Shift of the demand curve: caused by a change in ANYTHING ELSE - Income \(I\) changes - Price of another good \(P_y\) changes - Tastes change - Number of buyers changes A rightward shift = increase in demand at every price.
  17. What are substitute goods?Goods X and Y are substitutes if a rise in the price of Y increases the demand for X: \(\frac{\partial X^*}{\partial P_y} > 0\) When the price of Y rises, demand for X increases (consumers switch to X). Examples: Coke and Pepsi, butter and margarine, bus and taxi.
  18. What are complement goods?Goods X and Y are complements if they are consumed together, so a rise in the price of Y lowers the demand for X: \(\frac{\partial X^*}{\partial P_y} < 0\) When the price of Y rises, demand for X falls. Examples: coffee and creamer, cars and gasoline, smartphones and apps.
  19. Why are Giffen goods so rare in practice?Two conditions must hold simultaneously: 1. The good must be strongly inferior (large \(\partial X^*/\partial I < 0\)). 2. The good must occupy a very large share of the budget (so the income effect is large in absolute terms). In wealthy economies, no single good consumes most of the budget. Historical candidates: potatoes during the Irish Famine, low-quality staples in subsistence economies. Even then, empirical confirmation is difficult.
  20. For Cobb-Douglas \(U=X^{0.5}Y^{0.5}\), what is the optimal demand and Slutsky decomposition formula?Optimal: \(X^* = \frac{I}{2P_x}\), \(Y^* = \frac{I}{2P_y}\) After a price change from \(P_0\) to \(P_1\): \[X_A = \frac{I}{2P_0},\quad X_B = \frac{I}{2P_1},\quad X_C = \frac{I}{2P_0}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{P_0}{P_1}}\] \[\text{SE} = X_C - X_A = \frac{I}{2\sqrt{P_0 P_1}} - \frac{I}{2P_0}\] SE and IE are both negative when \(P_1 > P_0\) (normal good).
