Check yourself

20 flashcards on this week. They come back on a schedule: the ones you miss soon, the ones you know days later.

See all 20 cards with answers
  1. Distinguish accounting profit from economic profit. Why does the difference matter for decisions?Accounting profit = Total Revenue - Explicit costs (out-of-pocket payments). Economic profit = Total Revenue - Explicit costs - Implicit costs (opportunity costs of owner-supplied inputs). Why it matters: a firm can be profitable in an accounting sense but earning zero or negative economic profit - meaning the owner's resources are better deployed elsewhere. Economic profit = 0 means the firm earns exactly its opportunity cost - this is the long-run equilibrium condition in perfect competition.
  2. Define sunk costs and explain why they should not affect forward-looking decisions.Sunk costs are costs that have already been paid and cannot be recovered, regardless of what the firm does next. Examples: past R&D expenditure, a non-refundable deposit, the cost of a machine that cannot be resold. Why ignore them: a rational decision-maker compares future benefits to future costs only. Including sunk costs in the analysis leads to the 'sunk cost fallacy' - continuing a losing project because of past investment.
  3. Write the Total Cost (TC) decomposition for the short run. Define each component.\[TC = TFC + TVC\] Total Fixed Cost (TFC): Costs that do not vary with output q. Examples: capital rental, insurance. Must be paid even if q = 0. Total Variable Cost (TVC): Costs that rise with output. Examples: labor, raw materials. TVC(0) = 0. In the long run: ALL costs are variable, so there are no fixed costs.
  4. Define the isocost line. What does its slope represent?The isocost line shows all (K, L) combinations that cost the same total amount TC: \[TC = wL + vK \quad \Rightarrow \quad K = \frac{TC}{v} - \frac{w}{v}L\] Slope = -w/v (the ratio of input prices). K-intercept = TC/v (maximum capital if no labor). L-intercept = TC/w (maximum labor if no capital). Parallel shifts in the isocost (changing TC) correspond to different cost levels; rotations (changing w or v) correspond to changes in relative input prices.
  5. State the cost-minimization condition and explain why it must hold at the optimal input bundle.At the cost-minimizing bundle: \[MRTS_{LK} = \frac{MPL}{MPK} = \frac{w}{v}\] Intuition (bang-for-the-buck rule): the cost-minimizing firm equalizes the output per dollar spent on each input: \[\frac{MPL}{w} = \frac{MPK}{v}\] If MPL/w > MPK/v: shift spending from K to L (each dollar on L buys more output). If MPL/w < MPK/v: shift spending from L to K (each dollar on K buys more output). Converges at equality.
  6. List the five short-run cost curves and describe the relationship between them.1. TC (Total Cost) = TFC + TVC 2. AFC (Average Fixed Cost) = TFC/q - always declining 3. AVC (Average Variable Cost) = TVC/q - U-shaped 4. ATC (Average Total Cost) = TC/q = AFC + AVC - U-shaped, above AVC 5. SMC (Short-run Marginal Cost) = dTC/dq = dTVC/dq Key relationships: - SMC passes through the minimum of ATC - SMC passes through the minimum of AVC - As q increases, ATC-AVC = AFC narrows (AFC falls)
  7. Why does SMC (Short-run Marginal Cost) always pass through the minimum of ATC (Average Total Cost)?When MC < ATC: adding one more unit of output costs less than the current average, pulling the average down. When MC > ATC: adding one unit costs more than average, pulling the average up. When MC = ATC: ATC is at its minimum (neither rising nor falling). This is the same logic as class grades: if your new exam score (MC) is below your average (AC), your average falls; above it, your average rises; equal to it, your average is at a turning point. Mathematically: d(ATC)/dq = (MC - ATC)/q. Setting to zero: MC = ATC.
  8. What is the efficient scale of a firm? How does it appear on an AC diagram?The efficient scale is the output level q* that minimizes Average Total Cost (ATC). For AC = a/q + bq: \(q^* = \sqrt{a/b}\) On the AC diagram: it is the bottom of the U-shaped ATC curve, where SMC intersects ATC. Industry structure implication: - Low q*: many small firms can be efficient (competitive market) - High q*: few large firms are needed for efficiency (natural monopoly tendency)
  9. Define economies of scale and diseconomies of scale. How are they related to the LRAC?Economies of scale: Long-Run Average Cost (LRAC) falls as output rises. Each unit produced costs less on average as the firm grows. Diseconomies of scale: LRAC rises as output rises. The firm becomes less efficient at large scale (coordination problems, bureaucracy). On the LRAC diagram: - Downward-sloping segment = economies of scale - Upward-sloping segment = diseconomies of scale - Minimum LRAC = minimum efficient scale Note: economies of scale concern output changes, not input proportions.
  10. State the LRAC Envelope Theorem. Why is the long-run cost always at most equal to the short-run cost?The Long-Run Average Cost (LRAC) curve is the lower envelope of all Short-Run Average Cost (SRAC) curves (one for each plant size K). \[LRAC(q) \leq SRAC(q, K) \quad \forall K\] Why: in the long run, the firm can choose the optimal plant size K for any output q. In the short run, K is fixed, which may not be optimal. Extra flexibility always (weakly) reduces cost. Tangency: each SRAC is tangent to the LRAC from above at exactly one output level - the one for which that plant size is optimal.
  11. Common mistake: 'IRS (Increasing Returns to Scale) implies economies of scale, and DRS implies diseconomies of scale.' Is this always correct?Mostly yes, but the concepts are distinct. Returns to scale (production concept): how output responds when ALL inputs scale together. Economies of scale (cost concept): how average cost responds as output rises (with optimal input choices). For Cobb-Douglas: the relationship is direct - IRS gives falling LRAC (economies), CRS gives flat LRAC, DRS gives rising LRAC (diseconomies). But for non-Cobb-Douglas technologies, the relationship can be more complex. Economies of scale is the more general concept for cost theory.
  12. Explain how to derive the TC(q) curve from the isoquant map. What is being minimized?For each output level q: 1. Draw the isoquant for Q = q. 2. Find the isocost line (with slope -w/v) that is tangent to the isoquant from below. 3. The TC for that output = the cost of the tangent isocost = wL* + vK*. 4. Plot (q, TC) on a new graph. Repeat for all q levels - each tangency gives one point on the TC(q) curve. This is cost minimization: for each output level, we find the cheapest input combination.
  13. What happens to ATC when Total Fixed Cost (TFC) increases? What happens to SMC and AVC?When TFC rises: - ATC shifts UP (since ATC = TC/q = (TFC + TVC)/q, and TFC appears in the numerator) - AVC is UNCHANGED (TVC is unchanged, so AVC = TVC/q is unchanged) - SMC is UNCHANGED (SMC = dTC/dq = dTVC/dq, and TFC does not affect marginal cost) Intuition: fixed costs are fixed - they don't vary with output, so they don't affect the marginal decision. Only variable costs affect SMC and AVC.
  14. Describe what AFC (Average Fixed Cost) looks like and why it matters for the shape of ATC.AFC = TFC/q is a rectangular hyperbola - it falls continuously and approaches zero as q rises, but never reaches it. As q increases, AFC → 0, so ATC → AVC. This means the gap between ATC and AVC narrows as output rises - the two curves get closer together. At low output, ATC is much higher than AVC (large AFC contribution). At high output, ATC ≈ AVC (AFC is negligible). This spreading of fixed costs over more units is sometimes called 'spreading the overhead.'
  15. For a Leontief firm Q = min(L, K/2) with w = 4, v = 2. Find TC(q), LRTC, and LRAC.Optimal bundle: L = q and K = 2q (to satisfy Q = min(L, K/2) = q). \[TC = wL + vK = 4q + 2(2q) = 4q + 4q = 8q\] \[LRAC = \frac{TC}{q} = 8\] LRAC is constant - the firm has Constant Returns to Scale (CRS). Doubling both inputs doubles output and cost, so average cost stays flat.
  16. Define short-run costs vs long-run costs and explain the key SR/LR principle.Short run: At least one input (K) is fixed at K-bar. The firm can only adjust L. Long run: All inputs are variable. The firm chooses both K and L optimally. Key SR/LR principle: SRTC(q, K-bar) >= LRTC(q) for all q and all K-bar. Equality holds only where K-bar = K*(q) - i.e. where the fixed K happens to be the long-run optimal capital level for that output. Implication: the long-run always offers at least as low a cost as the short-run, because it provides an extra degree of freedom (choosing K).
  17. What does the LRAC envelope look like, and where is each SRAC tangent to it?The LRAC is the lower envelope of a family of U-shaped SRAC curves, one for each plant size K-bar. For each plant size K-bar, the SRAC is tangent to the LRAC at exactly one output level - the output for which K-bar is optimal. - If the economy-of-scale region (downward-sloping LRAC): the tangency is on the DOWNWARD-SLOPING part of the SRAC. - At minimum LRAC (efficient scale): tangency is at the MINIMUM of the corresponding SRAC. - If diseconomy-of-scale region: tangency is on the UPWARD-SLOPING part of the SRAC.
  18. For a Cobb-Douglas firm Q = K^0.5 L^0.5 with w and v, derive the long-run TC function.Cost minimization: MRTS = w/v => K/L = w/v => K = (w/v)L. Substitute into production function: Q = K^0.5 L^0.5 = ((w/v)L)^0.5 L^0.5 = (w/v)^0.5 L. So \(L^* = q(v/w)^{0.5}\) and \(K^* = q(w/v)^{0.5}\). \[TC = wL^* + vK^* = wq(v/w)^{0.5} + vq(w/v)^{0.5} = 2q\sqrt{wv}\] LRAC = 2sqrt(wv) - constant, confirming CRS (a+b=1).
  19. A firm's production function has IRS. Does this mean it should produce infinite output?No. IRS means average cost falls as output rises, which is an advantage. But the firm still faces: 1. Demand constraints: consumers are not willing to buy unlimited output at positive prices. 2. Eventually: most real-world production functions exhibit DRS at some large scale (managerial diseconomies, coordination failures). 3. Competition: in markets, prices can fall as quantity rises. IRS mainly implies: - Natural monopoly tendency (one large firm is more efficient than many small ones) - Minimum efficient scale is large relative to the market - The firm benefits from growing - but this is bounded by the market.
  20. State the 'bang-for-the-buck' rule and show how to apply it to reallocate inputs optimally.The firm minimizes cost by equalizing the output per dollar spent on each input: \[\frac{MPL}{w} = \frac{MPK}{v}\] If MPL/w > MPK/v: shift one dollar from K to L - output rises without changing cost. Repeat until equalized. Example: MPL = 12, MPK = 8, w = 4, v = 4. - MPL/w = 12/4 = 3, MPK/v = 8/4 = 2. MPL/w > MPK/v. - Hire more L, less K. As L rises, MPL falls; as K falls, MPK rises. - Stop when MPL/w = MPK/v.
