The Welfare Framework

CS/PS Triangle Anatomy

Consumer surplus (CS) is the area under demand and above the price; producer surplus (PS) is the area above supply and below the price. Total surplus TS = CS + PS is our welfare scorecard, and it is maximized exactly at the competitive equilibrium. In Fig 8.1 below a rule holds the price away from P* = $12, and a red deadweight-loss triangle appears: mutually beneficial trades that no longer happen.

Key insight: Every dollar of CS is a dollar of gain to buyers. Every dollar of PS is a dollar of gain to sellers. Total Surplus is our welfare scorecard, and only the competitive equilibrium drives DWL to zero.

Transfer vs. Destruction: The DWL Decomposition

When output is held below the efficient level Q* = 8, the lost consumer surplus splits into two categorically different pieces. A gold rectangle is transferred from buyers to the seller (redistributed, not lost). A red triangle is destroyed: it leaves the economy entirely. In Fig 8.2 below, split the loss at a held output yourself; after the reveal, move the restriction left from Q = 8 and watch the triangle outgrow the rectangle.

What happens under a monopoly? A monopolist restricts output below Q* = 8 to raise price above MC = $2. The gold rectangle is the transfer: consumers lose it, but the firm gains it, so society as a whole is not worse off, it is simply redistributed. The red triangle is the deadweight loss: this value is destroyed and no one receives it. As Q falls further below Q*, the triangle grows faster than the rectangle: the true social cost (DWL) outweighs the redistribution.
Key insight: DWL is not a transfer; it is value destroyed. No one in the economy receives it. Monopoly profit (the rectangle) is largely a redistribution; the triangle is the true social cost.

Policy Applications

Trade and Tariffs: Welfare Effects

Opening trade at a world price below the domestic equilibrium raises total surplus: consumers gain more than producers lose. A tariff reverses some of these gains. Fig 8.3 below asks where a tariff moves the price, production and consumption (the added cost of tariffs); after the reveal, its readout also compares free trade with no trade (the gains from trade). Model: D : P = 20−Q; S : P = 4+Q; domestic equilibrium P* = $12.

Key insight: Tariff revenue does NOT offset the DWL: the two red triangles are destroyed value, not a transfer. Free trade maximizes national welfare because the consumer gain exceeds the producer loss.

Monopoly

Monopoly Two-Step Rule: Guided Diagram

The single most-tested monopoly skill. Step 1: set MR = MC to find Q*M. Step 2: read the price up to the demand curve, never off MR. In Fig 8.4 below, draw MR and take both steps yourself; after the reveal, switch to the common mistake to see exactly how reading P from MR understates the price.

Profit = area of the gold rectangle. Look at the shaded gold box on the diagram. Its width = Q*M (units sold). Its height = P*M − MC (the markup per unit, how much more you charge than it costs to produce). Profit = width × height = Q*M × (P*M − MC). That is it. Profit is literally the area of that rectangle.
Key insight: Read the price from demand, not from MR. Setting MR = MC only locates the quantity; the demand curve tells you what buyers will actually pay for that quantity.

Competition vs. Monopoly Welfare Comparator

Fig 8.5 below puts the competitive outcome and the monopoly outcome on the same diagram. Under competition the whole CS triangle survives and DWL = 0. Under monopoly the CS triangle shrinks: part becomes the gold profit rectangle (transferred), and part becomes the red DWL triangle (destroyed). After the reveal, step MC to see how the deadweight loss scales with market power.

Key insight: The gold rectangle is a redistribution from consumers to the firm. The red triangle is the true social cost of monopoly, and CSmono + PSmono + DWL always equals TScomp.

Price Discrimination

Perfect Price Discrimination: Anatomy

Fig 8.7 below compares three states on one diagram (switch between them after the reveal). Under competition the CS triangle is large and DWL = 0. Under uniform monopoly CS shrinks, a gold profit rectangle appears, and a red DWL triangle is destroyed. Under perfect price discrimination the demand curve is sliced into thin slivers each priced at its buyer’s exact WTP: CS collapses to zero, but DWL also vanishes: every gain becomes profit.

Key insight: Perfect price discrimination is the only form of PD that eliminates deadweight loss. But it requires the seller to know every buyer’s WTP: allocatively efficient, yet perfectly inequitable.

Block Pricing: Approaching PPD from 2nd Degree

With a single uniform price (n = 1) the monopolist sells Q = 4 at $6 and leaves a full DWL triangle. Offering more price tiers lets the firm trace a step-function approximation of demand: each block is priced at the willingness to pay of its last unit, so every unit in the block is voluntarily bought. As the number of tiers rises, output expands toward Q* = 8, DWL shrinks (DWL = 32/(n+1)²), profit rises, and the outcome approaches perfect price discrimination.

Key insight: More price tiers → less deadweight loss. This is exactly why firms invest in market segmentation and versioning.

Market Separation: 3rd Degree PD

A monopolist selling in two separable markets sets MR₁ = MR₂ = MC to maximize total profit. Each market has its own demand and therefore its own price: the market where demand is less elastic always pays more. In Fig 8.9 below, set both prices yourself; the reveal draws MR1 and MR2 and shows where each meets MC.

Key insight: The price-discrimination rule is MR₁ = MR₂ = MC. Charge more in the less elastic market (lower price sensitivity = steeper demand = higher markup). Lerner index L = (P−MC)/P = −1/ε; higher |ε| (more elastic) → smaller markup → lower price.

Natural Monopoly Regulation Dilemma

With a large fixed cost, average cost declines over the whole range, so one firm serves the market most cheaply. But how to price it? Unregulated, the firm sets P > AC and creates DWL. Forcing P = MC is efficient but bankrupts the firm (it needs a subsidy). Forcing P = AC is the second-best compromise: zero profit, smaller DWL. After the reveal, switch between the regimes to compare. Model: $P = 20 - Q$, $TC = 50 + 2Q$, so $MC = 2$ and $AC = 50/Q + 2$.

Regulator’s dilemma: P = MC is efficient but causes losses that demand a subsidy. P = AC is second-best: the firm breaks even but some deadweight loss remains. There is no free lunch.

Two-Tier Pricing & Cross-Subsidization

A monopolist serving two consumer groups charges a high price PA to high-value customers (earning a green profit relative to AC) and a low price PR to price-sensitive customers (incurring a red loss relative to AC). High-tier profits cross-subsidize low-tier losses, allowing the firm to reach more consumers. Net profit = green area − red area. In Fig 8.11 below, find the lowest break-even PA; after the reveal, move PA and step PR to see when cross-subsidization works. Model: D: P = 20 − Q; TC = 50 + 2Q; AC = 50/Q + 2; MC = $2.

Key insight: Cross-subsidization requires AC(QR) to sit between PR and PA. High-tier markup covers fixed costs and subsidizes below-AC pricing for the low tier. When green > red, net profit > 0; when green = red, the firm exactly breaks even, identical to the AC-pricing solution in Fig 8.10. Fig 8.11 asks for that minimum PA at the current PR.

  1. What is consumer surplus? How is it measured on a supply-and-demand diagram?Consumer Surplus (CS) = Willingness to pay - Price paid. \[CS = WTP - P\] In aggregate: the area under the demand curve and above the market price. Intuition: CS is the 'deal' a buyer gets. The demand curve traces willingness to pay for each unit; the gap between WTP and the price actually paid is pure buyer gain. Example (campus coffee shop, price $4/latte): Alice (WTP $7) gains CS = $3. Ben (WTP $5.50) gains $1.50. Cara (WTP $3.80) does not buy, CS = 0. When price rises, CS falls for two reasons: each remaining buyer keeps less surplus, and some buyers stop buying entirely.
  2. What is producer surplus? Why is PS not the same thing as profit?Producer Surplus (PS) = Price received - Willingness to sell (MC). \[PS = P - MC\] In aggregate: the area above the supply curve and below the market price. PS vs profit: PS = price minus marginal (variable) cost. Profit = revenue minus TOTAL cost, including fixed costs. - When fixed costs are zero: PS = profit. - When fixed costs are present: profit = PS - fixed costs. Example: a baker with fixed costs of $100/day, MC = $3/loaf, price $7, 200 loaves sold. PS = (7-3) x 200 = $800. Profit = $800 - $100 = $700. Always check whether fixed costs are present before equating PS and profit.
  3. Define total surplus and economic efficiency. Why is the competitive equilibrium efficient?Total Surplus: TS = CS + PS. It is the monetary measure of the social value a market creates, so we can compare any two market outcomes on a common scale. Economic efficiency: an allocation is efficient if TS is maximized. Equivalently: no mutually beneficial trades remain unexploited. Why the competitive equilibrium is efficient: at (P*, Q*), every unit for which WTP > MC is traded, and no unit where WTP < MC is produced. Both conditions hold simultaneously, which is exactly why TS is maximized at Q*. Important: efficiency says nothing about how the pie is divided. Efficiency and equity are different dimensions.
  4. What is deadweight loss? Who 'receives' the DWL?Deadweight Loss (DWL): the reduction in total surplus caused by a market distortion, an output level different from Q*. \[DWL = TS_{Q^*} - TS_{actual}\] DWL is the value of mutually beneficial trades that do not occur. It is captured by NOBODY: not consumers, not producers, not the government. It is value destroyed outright, not a transfer. Geometrically: the triangle between the demand and supply curves, between the restricted quantity Q1 and the efficient quantity Q*. Note: overproduction also destroys value (units where MC > WTP). Q* is the unique output that maximizes TS.
  5. D: P = 12 - Q and S: P = 2 + Q. Find the equilibrium, then compute CS, PS, and TS.Equilibrium: 12 - Q = 2 + Q, so Q* = 5 and P* = $7. CS = area under demand, above price: CS = (1/2)(12 - 7)(5) = $12.50 PS = area above supply, below price: PS = (1/2)(7 - 2)(5) = $12.50 TS = CS + PS = $25. Check with the total-surplus triangle: TS = (1/2)(12 - 2)(5) = 25. Correct. This is the chapter's benchmark technique: with linear curves, every welfare quantity is a triangle or rectangle you can compute from intercepts and the equilibrium point.
  6. A monopoly raises price from P* to P_M. Decompose the consumers' loss into its two parts. Which part is DWL?The CS loss has TWO parts: 1. The rectangle (transfer): consumers who still buy now pay P_M instead of P*. That money goes to the seller as profit. It is redistributed, NOT lost to society. 2. The triangle (destruction): consumers priced out of the market entirely. Their would-be surplus vanishes and nobody receives it. This triangle is the DWL. Only the triangle is deadweight loss. In the chapter benchmark (D: P = 10 - Q, MC = 4): the transfer rectangle = $9 (monopoly profit) and the destroyed triangle = $4.50 (DWL). Always decompose a CS loss into transfer + destruction before calling any of it DWL.
  7. When a country opens to imports at world price P_W < P_D, who wins, who loses, and what happens to total surplus?Opening trade when P_W < P_D: - Domestic price falls from P_D to P_W. - Domestic demand rises; domestic supply falls; imports fill the gap. - CS rises (consumers pay less). - PS falls (domestic producers sell less at a lower price). - TS RISES: the consumer gain exceeds the producer loss. The net gain is the gains-from-trade triangle: \[\tfrac{1}{2}(P_D - P_W)(Q_1 - Q_2)\] where Q1 is post-trade demand and Q2 is post-trade domestic supply. Political economy: producers lobby for protection because their losses are concentrated; consumer gains are large but diffuse.
  8. Tariff welfare accounting. D: P = 30 - Q, S: P = 6 + 0.5Q, world price P_W = $10, tariff t = $2. Compute the change in CS, PS, government revenue, and TS.Tariff raises the domestic price from P_W = 10 to P_R = 12. At P_W = 10: Q_s = 8, Q_d = 20, imports = 12. At P_R = 12: Q_s = 12, Q_d = 18, imports = 6. ΔCS = -(price rise) x (average quantity demanded) = -(12 - 10) x (20 + 18)/2 = -$38 (a trapezoid: consumers pay more AND buy less). ΔPS = +$20 (domestic producers gain). Gov revenue = t x imports = 2 x 6 = $12. ΔTS = -38 + 20 + 12 = -$6. That -$6 is the two DWL triangles: one from inefficient expansion of domestic production, one from reduced consumption. Net: tariffs reduce total surplus.
  9. Common mistake: 'Tariff revenue offsets the deadweight loss.' Why is this wrong?Wrong. Government tariff revenue is a TRANSFER (from consumers to the government) and is already counted inside TS. The two DWL triangles represent value that is destroyed even after counting every dollar of government revenue: 1. Production triangle: domestic firms produce units whose MC exceeds the world price (wasted resources). 2. Consumption triangle: consumers stop buying units they valued above the world price. Even if the government refunded every dollar of tariff revenue to consumers, the DWL triangles would remain as lost value. DWL and tariff revenue are separate components: revenue is redistribution, DWL is destruction.
  10. What is a monopoly? What keeps competitors out?Monopoly: a market with a single seller of a good for which there are no close substitutes. The firm IS the market: it faces the entire downward-sloping market demand curve directly, so it is a price MAKER (chooses P), unlike a competitive price taker. Barriers to entry keep rivals out. Two categories: - Technical: ownership of a key resource; declining average cost over all output (natural monopoly); superior proprietary technology. - Legal: patents, copyrights, exclusive government franchises. Example: a pharmaceutical patent (typically 20 years) lets the firm price far above MC; after expiry, generic entry pushes price toward MC and DWL shrinks dramatically.
  11. State the monopoly two-step rule and apply it to the benchmark: D: P = 10 - Q, MC = AC = $4.Monopoly Two-Step Rule: 1. Find Q*_M: set MR = MC and solve for output. 2. Find P*_M: read the DEMAND curve at Q*_M. The monopolist never sets P = MC. Benchmark: P = 10 - Q, so MR = 10 - 2Q. Step 1: 10 - 2Q = 4 gives Q*_M = 3. Step 2: P*_M = 10 - 3 = $7. Profit = Q*_M x (P*_M - AC) = 3 x (7 - 4) = $9. Compare the competitive outcome: P = MC gives Q_PC = 6, P_PC = $4. The monopolist halves output and nearly doubles price.
  12. Why is MR < P for a monopolist? Derive the MR curve for linear demand P = a - bQ.The monopolist must lower price on ALL units to sell one more: \[MR = P + Q\frac{dP}{dQ} < P \quad (since\; dP/dQ < 0)\] Two effects of selling one more unit: 1. Gain: revenue from the new unit (= P). 2. Loss: the price cut applied to every unit already being sold (= Q x dP/dQ). For linear demand P = a - bQ: TR = aQ - bQ^2, so \[MR = a - 2bQ\] Same y-intercept as demand (a), twice the slope (-2b vs -b), hits zero at Q = a/(2b), half the demand choke quantity. At MR = 0, total revenue is maximized. Contrast: a competitive price taker has MR = P (no price effect).
  13. Common mistake: a student sets MR = MC, finds Q*_M, and reports P*_M = MC. What went wrong?They forgot Step 2 of the two-step rule. MR = MC pins down the QUANTITY only. The price comes from reading the DEMAND curve at that quantity: \[P^*_M = D(Q^*_M)\] Stopping at Step 1 and reporting P = MC gives the COMPETITIVE price, not the monopoly price. Benchmark check (D: P = 10 - Q, MC = 4): Step 1 gives Q*_M = 3. The wrong answer reports P = $4. The right answer reads demand: P*_M = 10 - 3 = $7. Memory hook: Step 1 finds the quantity, Step 2 finds the price. The demand curve determines what price the market will bear at the chosen quantity. Never skip Step 2.
  14. Common mistake: confusing the MR curve with the demand curve. How do you tell them apart, and what error does the confusion cause?Both start at the same y-intercept (a), which is why students mix them up. But: - Demand shows what consumers PAY per unit at each quantity. - MR shows the ADDITIONAL revenue from selling one more unit. - For P = a - bQ, the MR curve is twice as steep: MR = a - 2bQ. The error: setting P = MR (or drawing MR as if it were demand) gives the wrong Q*_M. Also wrong: setting demand = MC ('P = MC') to find monopoly output. That gives the competitive quantity. Correct procedure: intersect MR with MC for quantity, then jump UP to the demand curve for price.
  15. Fill in the three-scenario welfare table for the benchmark (D: P = 10 - Q, MC = 4): competition, uniform monopoly, and perfect price discrimination.CS / Profit / TS / DWL: Perfect competition (Q = 6, P = 4): CS = 18, Profit = 0, TS = 18, DWL = 0 Uniform monopoly (Q = 3, P = 7): CS = 4.5, Profit = 9, TS = 13.5, DWL = 4.5 Perfect price discrimination (Q = 6): CS = 0, Profit = 18, TS = 18, DWL = 0 Verify: CS_PC = (1/2)(10-4)(6) = 18. CS_M = (1/2)(10-7)(3) = 4.5. DWL = (1/2)(7-4)(6-3) = 4.5. Key lesson: competition and PPD TIE on efficiency (TS = 18, DWL = 0) but are polar opposites on equity. Competition gives the surplus to consumers; PPD gives it all to the producer. Uniform monopoly is neither efficient nor equitable.
  16. Define the Lerner Index. A monopolist has L = 0.4 and P = $50. What is its marginal cost?Lerner Index: \[L = \frac{P - MC}{P} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_d}\] L is the fraction of price that is markup above marginal cost. - L = 0 for a competitive firm (P = MC). - L tends to 1 as market power becomes extreme. - At the monopoly optimum, L = -1/elasticity: less elastic demand means a higher markup. Solving: 0.4 = (50 - MC)/50, so MC = 50 x 0.6 = $30. Real-world anchors: competitive wheat farming L near 0; brand-name pharmaceuticals under patent L of 0.5 to 0.9; Gleevec (imatinib) priced around $92,000/year against manufacturing cost under $1,000/year implies L > 0.98.
  17. What two conditions must hold for a firm to price discriminate?Price discrimination: charging different prices for identical (or near-identical) units of output to different buyers. Two necessary conditions: 1. Market power: the seller must face downward-sloping demand. A price taker cannot price discriminate; the market sets its price. 2. No arbitrage: the seller must be able to prevent low-price buyers from reselling to high-price buyers. Without this, the price differential collapses as resellers undercut the high price. Example of arbitrage prevention: airline tickets are non-transferable, require ID, and carry advance-purchase restrictions, so a leisure traveler cannot resell a cheap seat to a last-minute business traveler.
  18. What is perfect (first-degree) price discrimination? Is it efficient? Is it equitable?Perfect Price Discrimination (PPD): each consumer is charged exactly their willingness to pay. Every unit sells at the price indicated by the demand curve. Consequences: - CS = 0 (no consumer keeps any surplus). - The monopolist sells every unit with WTP >= MC, so output reaches the competitive quantity Q_PC. - DWL = 0. All surplus accrues to the monopolist as profit. Efficient? YES: the right quantity is produced and TS is maximized (ties with perfect competition). Equitable? NO: consumers get nothing; the seller eats the entire pie. This is Week 8's central lesson: efficiency and equity are distinct dimensions. PPD maximizes the pie while distributing it as unequally as possible.
  19. What is second-degree price discrimination? Give two common forms and a real-world example.Second-degree PD: charging different prices based on the QUANTITY purchased or the VERSION chosen, not on the buyer's identity. The firm cannot observe individual WTP, so it designs a menu that induces buyers to self-select. Two common forms: 1. Quantity discounts: lower per-unit price for larger purchases (wholesale clubs, utility block pricing). 2. Versioning: multiple product tiers at different prices, so high-WTP buyers self-select into the expensive version. Example: Microsoft Office 'Home' at $100 vs 'Business' at $300. The extra features cost nearly nothing to provide; the price gap exists to sort buyers. The menu does the work. Welfare note: second-degree PD typically increases output relative to uniform monopoly pricing, so it REDUCES DWL, though CS stays below the competitive level.
  20. Third-degree PD: D1: Q1 = 100 - P1, D2: Q2 = 100 - 2P2, MC = $20. Find each market's price. Which market pays more, and why?Rule: act as a separate monopolist in each market (set MR_i = MC), then read each demand curve. Market 1: P1 = 100 - Q1, MR1 = 100 - 2Q1 = 20 gives Q1* = 40, P1* = $60. Profit = (60-20)(40) = $1,600. Market 2: P2 = 50 - Q2/2, MR2 = 50 - Q2 = 20 gives Q2* = 30, P2* = $35. Profit = (35-20)(30) = $450. Total discriminating profit = $2,050 vs roughly $1,633 with a single uniform price: discrimination adds about $417. Market 1 pays more because its demand is LESS elastic at the optimum. Pricing rule: charge a higher price in the less elastic (captive) market, a lower price in the more elastic market. Airlines: business travelers (must travel, employer pays) face the high fare; flexible leisure travelers get the low one.
  21. What is a natural monopoly, and what is the regulator's dilemma in pricing it?Natural monopoly: an industry where ONE firm can produce the market's total output at lower cost than two or more firms could. This happens when average cost declines over the entire relevant output range, typically huge fixed costs plus low marginal costs (water networks, electricity grids, broadband). The regulator's dilemma: - P = MC (marginal-cost pricing): efficient, DWL = 0, BUT the firm makes a loss (P < AC) and needs a subsidy to survive. - P = AC (average-cost pricing): the firm breaks even, no subsidy needed, BUT some DWL remains. This is the 'second-best' solution. - Unregulated monopoly: profitable but the most inefficient. Most public utilities are regulated at average-cost pricing: viability without subsidy, at the cost of some efficiency. There is no free lunch.
  22. What is rent-seeking? Why does it mean the standard DWL triangle understates the social cost of monopoly?Rent-seeking: resources spent to OBTAIN or MAINTAIN monopoly power: lobbying, patent litigation, regulatory capture, predatory pricing against potential entrants. These expenditures are socially wasteful: they redistribute profit toward the monopolist but create no new value. Why DWL understates the cost: in the extreme, a firm might spend up to its ENTIRE expected monopoly profit on rent-seeking. Then the true social cost is the DWL triangle PLUS the profit rectangle, a far larger number than the standard DWL calculation suggests. In the benchmark: standard DWL = $4.50, but with full rent-seeking the social cost could approach $4.50 + $9 = $13.50.
  23. True or False: 'Monopoly just transfers surplus from consumers to the firm, so society as a whole is no worse off.'False. The statement describes only the rectangle, not the triangle. Decompose what consumers lose when output falls from Q_PC to Q_M: 1. The profit rectangle IS a pure transfer: consumers pay more, the firm earns more, society's total is unchanged by this part. 2. The DWL triangle is NOT a transfer: it is the value of trades between Q_M and Q_PC where WTP > MC, trades that simply never happen. No one captures this value. Benchmark numbers: TS falls from $18 (competition) to $13.5 (monopoly). The missing $4.5 went to nobody. This is exactly why antitrust authorities (DOJ, European Commission) evaluate mergers with this framework: blocking a merger preserves efficiency, not just 'fairness.'
  24. Common mistake: 'Perfect price discrimination is better than uniform monopoly, full stop.' What is missing from this claim?The claim only looks at efficiency and ignores equity. What is TRUE: PPD achieves the efficient quantity (Q_PC), eliminates the DWL, and maximizes TS, tying with perfect competition on efficiency. What is MISSING: under PPD, consumer surplus is ZERO. Every consumer pays exactly their WTP, so consumers are at the worst possible outcome even though society's pie is at its maximum. A correct answer must acknowledge both dimensions: - Efficiency: PPD ties with competition (TS maximized, DWL = 0). - Equity: PPD is the worst possible outcome for consumers. Whether PPD is 'better' than uniform monopoly depends on whether the policymaker weighs total surplus or its distribution. Efficiency is not the same thing as desirability.
