Quantity Competition

Cournot & Stackelberg on the Best-Response Diagram

Market demand is \(P = a - bQ\) with \(MC = 0\). Each firm’s reaction curve gives its profit-maximizing output as a function of the rival’s. Firm B always best-responds along its own reaction curve, but only where the two curves cross is A also best-responding, the Cournot-Nash equilibrium. Away from that crossing the market is not at rest: the firms take turns best-responding and the staircase spirals into the Cournot point. A Stackelberg leader instead commits first and picks the point on B’s curve it likes best, where one of its iso-profit curves just touches B’s curve. In Fig 10.1 below you draw B’s reaction curve and find that point; afterwards you can change the market demand, move A’s output and watch the firms adjust.

Commitment is power. The Stackelberg leader produces more than its Cournot quantity and earns a premium purely by moving first, forcing the follower down its reaction curve. But the commitment must be both observable and irreversible, a threat the rival does not believe changes nothing. And notice the adjustment path: from any starting point the best-response dynamics converge back to the same Cournot crossing, which is what makes it a stable equilibrium.

Location Competition

The Hotelling Beach: Why Competitors Cluster

Two vendors sit on a one-mile beach; sunbathers are spread evenly and always walk to the nearer vendor. The dashed line is the midpoint that splits the market. Whoever slides toward the center captures a bigger slice, so both keep creeping inward until they sit side by side at \(0.5\), the principle of minimum differentiation. In Fig 10.2 below, find vendor A’s best spot against a fixed B and predict where both end up; afterwards you can move either vendor. The socially efficient spread (the quarter-points) minimizes walking, but it is not a Nash equilibrium.

Minimum differentiation. Both vendors converge to the center even though the quarter-points would minimize total travel. Each firm cares only about its own market share, not total travel cost, so private incentives and social efficiency pull in opposite directions, which is why competitors so often cluster.

How Many Firms?

n-Firm Cournot: From Monopoly to Competition

With \(n\) identical firms and \(P = 120 - Q,\ MC = 0\), each firm produces \(q^{*}=\tfrac{120}{n+1}\) and the market price is \(P^{*}=\tfrac{120}{n+1}\). One firm is the monopoly (\(P=\$60\)), two is the Cournot duopoly ($40), and as \(n\) grows the price slides down toward marginal cost. Cournot nests monopoly and perfect competition as its two limiting cases. In Fig 10.3 below, predict the price with one firm, with several, and where it heads as more firms enter.

Competition is a limit, not a switch. The same model spans the whole spectrum: market power fades smoothly as firms are added, and price approaches marginal cost only as \(n\to\infty\). This is why the number of competitors, not just their existence, shapes how close a market gets to the efficient outcome.

The Strategic Variable Matters

Four Models, One Market

The same duopoly (\(P = 120 - Q,\ MC = 0\)) yields four different answers depending on what firms choose and when. Cournot firms pick quantities at once; a Stackelberg leader commits first; colluding firms split the monopoly output; Bertrand firms set prices and compete it away to marginal cost. In Fig 10.4 below, place all four on the demand curve; afterwards pick a model to see total output, price and industry profit worked out step by step, with profit, consumer surplus and deadweight loss shaded on the demand diagram.

Not just how many firms, but how they compete. Collusion reproduces the monopoly ($3,600 industry profit); two Bertrand price-setters wipe profit out entirely ($0); Cournot and Stackelberg sit in between. The strategic variable, quantity versus price, and the timing, simultaneous versus sequential, matter as much as the number of competitors.

Bertrand: The Incentive to Undercut

With identical products buyers go to whoever is cheapest. Market demand is \(Q = 120 - P\) and \(MC = \$20\). Your profit depends on your price relative to the rival’s: price above the rival and you sell nothing; match and you split the market; undercut (while staying above cost) and you capture the whole market. Your best response to a fixed rival price is to shave just below it (or, if the rival is above the monopoly price of $70, simply charge $70), which is what drives price down to marginal cost. In Fig 10.5 below, draw your profit at every price and pick your best one; afterwards you can move both prices or start a price war.

The paradox: two firms are enough. Because the best response is always to undercut by a penny, any price above marginal cost is unstable, and price collapses to \(MC\) with zero profit, the perfectly competitive outcome from just two firms. The paradox breaks only when capacity is limited, products differ, or the game repeats.

  1. What is a dominant strategy? How does it simplify finding a Nash equilibrium?A dominant strategy yields a strictly higher payoff than any other strategy, no matter what the opponent does. A rational player always plays it, so you need no reasoning about the opponent's choice. Two anchoring facts: 1. Dominant strategies do not exist in every game. 2. When every player has one, the Nash equilibrium is simply the profile of dominant strategies. Advertising example: Coke vs Pepsi each earns more by advertising regardless of the rival (it steals share when the rival is silent, and avoids losing share when the rival advertises). Advertising is dominant for both, and the Nash outcome (Advertise, Advertise) is Pareto-dominated by (Don't, Don't), a direct parallel to the prisoner's dilemma.
  2. Describe the Cournot model. What does it mean that quantities are strategic substitutes?In Cournot competition, firms choose QUANTITIES simultaneously, each treating the rival's output as given. Firm A maximizes pi_A = (120 - q_A - q_B) q_A, giving the best-response (reaction) function: \[q_A^{*}(q_B) = (120 - q_B)/2\] Strategic substitutes: A's optimal output FALLS as q_B rises (the slope of the reaction function is negative). More rival output means A optimally produces less. Equilibrium (P = 120 - Q, MC = 0): q_A = q_B = 40, Q = 80, P = $40, pi = $1,600 each. Output lies strictly between monopoly (60) and competition (120): more firms means more output and a lower price.
  3. What is backward induction, and what is the subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE) it produces?Backward induction solves a sequential game by starting at the terminal nodes and working backward, choosing the payoff-maximizing action at each decision node. The result is the subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE): a Nash equilibrium that prescribes optimal play at EVERY subgame, so it eliminates equilibria sustained by non-credible threats. Entry game: an incumbent's threat to 'fight' any entrant is not credible, because once entry occurs, accommodating (payoff 2) beats fighting (payoff 0). Anticipating this, the entrant enters. SPNE = (Enter, Accommodate). Credibility requires commitment: a verbal threat is empty; only a pre-commitment (e.g. building excess capacity) that makes fighting a genuine best response can deter entry.
  4. Describe the Stackelberg model. Why does the leader gain a first-mover advantage?In Stackelberg competition the leader (Firm A) commits to a quantity FIRST, and the follower (Firm B) observes it and best-responds. Substituting B's reaction q_B = (120 - q_A)/2 into A's profit gives pi_A = (60 - q_A/2) q_A, maximized at q_A = 60; the follower then produces q_B = 30. Outcome: Q = 90, P = $30, pi_A = $1,800, pi_B = $900. The leader produces 50% more than its Cournot quantity (40) and earns a $200 premium over Cournot ($1,600). Why it works: by committing to a large output the leader forces the follower onto a lower branch of its reaction function. The commitment must be both OBSERVABLE and IRREVERSIBLE to confer the advantage.
  5. State the Bertrand model and the Bertrand paradox. Which three assumptions make the paradox break down?In Bertrand competition, firms set PRICES simultaneously. With identical products, unlimited capacity, and one-shot play, the unique Nash equilibrium is P_A = P_B = MC with ZERO profit: either firm can capture the whole market by undercutting a cent, and that logic drives price down to marginal cost. The Bertrand paradox: just TWO firms suffice to reproduce the perfectly competitive outcome. Three assumptions that often fail (and restore positive margins): 1. Capacity constraints: a small firm cannot serve the whole market, so undercutting is limited. 2. Product differentiation: loyal customers let a firm price above MC. 3. Repeated interaction: the threat of future price wars sustains tacit collusion. If a near-identical duopoly still prices above MC, at least one assumption is broken. Identifying which is more useful than memorising the paradox.
  6. State the Hotelling model result and the principle of minimum differentiation. Is the equilibrium efficient?Two vendors on a 1-mile beach with uniformly distributed consumers each choose a location; a consumer visits the nearer vendor, so vendor A's share is (a + b)/2. Moving toward the center RAISES a vendor's share (the derivative of A's share with respect to a is 1/2 > 0), so both creep inward until they sit side by side at the middle: \[a^{*} = b^{*} = 1/2\] This is the principle of minimum differentiation. Efficient? No. The socially efficient locations are the quarter-points (0.25, 0.75), which minimize total travel, but that is NOT a Nash equilibrium. Private incentives and social efficiency diverge. Same logic explains why petrol stations cluster, political parties converge to the center, and rival smartphones look alike.
  7. Derive and verify the Cournot-Nash duopoly. P = 120 - Q, MC = 0. Find each firm's output, and check that (40, 40) is a Nash equilibrium.Firm A maximizes pi_A = (120 - q_A - q_B) q_A. First-order condition: 120 - 2q_A - q_B = 0, so q_A = (120 - q_B)/2 (the reaction function). By symmetry q_A = q_B. Substitute: q = (120 - q)/2, so 2q = 120 - q, q = 40. Equilibrium: q_A = q_B = 40, Q = 80, P = $40, pi_i = (40)(40) = $1,600. Check no profitable deviation: if A deviates to q_A = 50 (with q_B = 40), Q = 90, P = 30, pi_A = 30 x 50 = $1,500 < $1,600. Deviating lowers profit, so (40, 40) is a Nash equilibrium.
  8. State the n-firm Cournot formula (P = 120 - Q, MC = 0) and show it nests monopoly and perfect competition.With n identical firms: \[q_i^{*} = \frac{120}{n+1}, \quad Q^{*} = \frac{120n}{n+1}, \quad P^{*} = \frac{120}{n+1}\] Limiting cases: - n = 1: q = 60, Q = 60, P = $60 (monopoly). - n = 2: q = 40, Q = 80, P = $40 (duopoly). - n = 3: q = 30, Q = 90, P = $30. - n to infinity: P to $0 = MC, Q to 120 (perfect competition). So the Cournot model nests monopoly (n = 1) and perfect competition (n to infinity) as the two extremes: more firms drive price toward marginal cost. This is exactly why fares on two-carrier airline routes exceed those on routes with three or more carriers.
  9. First-mover advantage. Stackelberg with P = 120 - Q, MC = 0. Confirm the leader earns $1,800 and quantify the premium over Cournot.Leader commits q_A = 60; follower best-responds q_B = (120 - 60)/2 = 30. Q = 90, P = 120 - 90 = $30. pi_A (leader) = 30 x 60 = $1,800. pi_B (follower) = 30 x 30 = $900. Compare to Cournot ($1,600 each): - Leader gains: 1,800 - 1,600 = $200 premium, the pure value of committing first. - Follower loses: 900 vs 1,600, being forced to follow is costly. Note total industry profit ($2,700) is lower than Cournot's ($3,200): the leader's aggression drives price down. The advantage is distributional, taken from the follower, not created from thin air.
  10. Bertrand with demand. Two identical firms, MC = $10, market demand Q = 500 - 5P. Find the equilibrium price, quantity, and profit, and contrast with monopoly.Bertrand equilibrium: price is driven to marginal cost. P^{*} = MC = $10. Q = 500 - 5(10) = 450 (split between the two firms). Profit = $0 each (price equals marginal cost). Contrast with a monopolist on the same demand: P = 100 - Q/5, so MR = 100 - 2Q/5. Set MR = MC = 10: 100 - 2Q/5 = 10, so 2Q/5 = 90, Q = 225, P = $55, with large positive profit. Headline: two Bertrand price-setters wipe out the entire monopoly profit. The number of firms is not the only thing that matters, the strategic variable (price vs quantity) does.
  11. Differentiated Bertrand. Firm A faces q_A = 60 - 2P_A + P_B, MC = 0. Derive A's best-response price and solve the symmetric equilibrium. Why is price above MC?Firm A maximizes pi_A = P_A(60 - 2P_A + P_B). First-order condition: d/dP_A = 60 - 4P_A + P_B = 0, so \[P_A^{*} = \frac{60 + P_B}{4}\] (prices are strategic complements: A raises its price when B does). Symmetric equilibrium, set P_A = P_B = P: P = (60 + P)/4, so 4P = 60 + P, 3P = 60, P = $20 > MC = 0. Why above MC: with differentiated products, a firm pricing slightly above the rival keeps its loyal customers rather than losing the whole market. Undercutting no longer captures everything, so the destructive Bertrand logic is blunted and margins survive. This is exactly the 'differentiation' escape from the paradox.
  12. Compare the four oligopoly models on the SAME market (P = 120 - Q, MC = 0): Cournot, Stackelberg, collusion, Bertrand.Outputs / total Q, P / profits: Cournot (simultaneous quantities): q = 40, 40; Q = 80, P = $40; pi = $1,600 / $1,600. Stackelberg (A leads): q = 60, 30; Q = 90, P = $30; pi = $1,800 / $900. Collusion (cartel, splits monopoly): q = 30, 30; Q = 60, P = $60; pi = $1,800 / $1,800. Bertrand (prices): q = 60, 60; Q = 120, P = $0; pi = $0 / $0. The punchline: the SAME market gives four different answers depending on what firms choose (quantity vs price vs location) and when (simultaneous vs sequential). Collusion is best for the firms and worst for consumers; Bertrand is the reverse; Cournot and Stackelberg sit in between.
  13. Common mistake: setting MR = MC on total output Q in a Cournot problem. Why is that wrong?Wrong. Setting MR = MC on total output Q solves the MONOPOLY problem (one decision-maker choosing all output), not the Cournot problem. In Cournot, each firm differentiates its OWN profit (P - MC) q_i, holding the RIVAL's quantity fixed: d/dq_A of [(120 - q_A - q_B) q_A] = 120 - 2q_A - q_B = 0. That gives the reaction function q_A = (120 - q_B)/2, not the monopoly answer. The difference: a monopolist internalizes the price effect on ALL units; a Cournot firm only accounts for the effect on its OWN units, treating the rival's output as a fixed background. That is exactly why Cournot output (80) exceeds monopoly output (60).
  14. Common mistake: confusing the Stackelberg leader's and follower's outputs. What is the correct assignment and what error follows?The LEADER commits to the LARGER quantity (60); the FOLLOWER best-responds with the smaller (30). Reversing them reverses the profits. Why the leader produces more: by committing first to a large, irreversible output, it forces the follower down its reaction function. The follower's best response to q_A = 60 is (120 - 60)/2 = 30. If you mistakenly give the leader 30 and the follower 60, you get pi_leader = $900 and pi_follower = $1,800, exactly backwards, implying (falsely) that moving first is a disadvantage. Memory hook: the leader's whole advantage is producing MORE than it would in Cournot (60 > 40); the follower produces LESS (30 < 40).
  15. Common mistake: 'Two firms can never produce a competitive outcome.' Why is this false?False. Under BERTRAND competition, two identical price-setting firms drive price all the way to marginal cost, exactly the perfectly competitive result, with zero profit. The reasoning: any price above MC invites the rival to undercut by a cent and capture the entire market, so undercutting continues until P = MC. Lesson: the number of firms is NOT the only determinant of competitiveness. The STRATEGIC VARIABLE matters: - Two quantity-setters (Cournot): price above MC, positive profit. - Two price-setters (Bertrand): price = MC, zero profit. Same two firms, same demand, opposite outcomes, purely because of what they choose. This is why 'how many firms?' is the wrong first question for a regulator; 'how do they compete?' is the right one.
  16. Common mistake: treating every Nash equilibrium of a game as subgame-perfect. Why can sequential play eliminate a Nash equilibrium?Not every Nash equilibrium survives backward induction. Some are sustained by NON-CREDIBLE threats, and the SPNE refinement discards them. Example: A moves first (Top/Bottom), B responds. Payoffs (A, B): Top to (Left 3,9)(Right 1,8); Bottom to (Left 0,0)(Right 2,1). The simultaneous game has a Nash equilibrium at (Bottom, Right), but it relies on B threatening 'Right if you play Top' (leaving A with 1 instead of 3) when in fact, once A plays Top, B prefers Left (9 > 8). Backward induction: if A plays Top, B picks Left (9 > 8), A gets 3; if A plays Bottom, B picks Right, A gets 2. A compares 3 vs 2 and plays Top. SPNE = (Top, Left). The (Bottom, Right) equilibrium is eliminated because it embeds a threat B would never carry out.
  17. Common mistake: expecting Hotelling firms to spread out to the efficient locations. Where do they actually locate, and why the divergence?Students expect the two firms to spread to the quarter-points (0.25 and 0.75), which minimize total consumer travel and are socially efficient. They do NOT. The Nash equilibrium is BOTH at the center (a = b = 1/2): minimum differentiation. From any pair of interior locations, either firm can raise its market share by edging toward the center, so the only stable configuration is both at the middle. Why the divergence: each firm cares only about its own market share, not total travel cost. Moving inward steals share from the rival even as it raises average travel for consumers. Private incentives (share) and social efficiency (minimizing travel) pull in opposite directions. This externality, one firm's location choice imposing travel costs on the other's would-be customers, is the whole point of the model.
