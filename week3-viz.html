§ 3.1

Two-Panel Demand Derivation

Every price of X pins down one optimal bundle in the utility maximization panel. Now let's change the price of X and see what changes. As the price falls, the budget line pivots outward, the consumer reaches a new indifference curve, and a new optimal point emerges. That bundle's X-coordinate is then plotted in the demand panel below it, building the demand curve one point at a time. § 3.2

Slutsky Decomposition Explorer

Any price change can be split into a substitution effect (SE), moving along the original IC at new prices, and an income effect (IE), shifting between ICs due to changed real income. In Fig 3.2 below, build the compensated budget line yourself and find C; after the reveal, change the new price and watch the decomposition update live.

Remember: The compensated budget line has the new slope (-PX,new/PY) but provides just enough income to maintain the original utility UA. Point C is its tangency with the original IC. The SE always moves X opposite to the price: negative when PX rises, positive when it falls (as at the default here).
§ 3.3

Income Effects & Demand Shifts

A change in income shifts the entire demand curve. For a normal good, rising income shifts demand rightward; for an inferior good, rising income shifts demand leftward. Predict both moves in Fig 3.3 below, then switch between the two types of good and watch the income expansion path trace out.

§ 3.4

Shift vs. Movement Drill

The demand curve for X plots PX on the vertical axis and QX on the horizontal axis, and everything else is held fixed. So a change in PX itself simply slides the consumer along the existing curve (a movement). Any other change (income, the price of another good, tastes, number of buyers) alters how much X is demanded at every price, which shifts the entire curve. Fig 3.4 below gives you one scenario at a time: show it on the graph as a movement or a shift, and the explanation tells you why.

§ 3.5

Cross-Price & Demand Function Explorer

Two types of price effects show up on a demand diagram, and they look completely different. Moving the own price (PX) traces a path along the existing demand curve: quantity adjusts, but the curve itself stays put. Changing the cross price (PY, the price of another good) shifts the entire curve: at every possible PX, demand for X is now higher or lower than before.

Why the difference? The demand curve for X already embeds a fixed PY. When PY changes, that embedded assumption breaks: you need a whole new curve. The direction of the shift reveals the relationship: if X and Y are substitutes (think Pepsi vs Coke), a higher PY makes X look relatively cheaper and shifts demand for X rightward. If they are complements (think coffee and milk), a higher PY makes the combination more expensive and shifts demand for X leftward.

Try this in Fig 3.5 below once you have answered: set the relationship to Substitutes, then hold PX fixed and raise PY. Watch the demand curve shift right, meaning at the same PX, consumers now want more X because Y just got more expensive. Then switch to Complements and repeat: the shift reverses.

Key distinction: Changing PX causes a movement along the demand curve (the current point slides). Changing PY or I shifts the entire curve.
