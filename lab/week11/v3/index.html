Figure 11.1

Pollution and the Pigouvian tax

11.1 Pigouvian tax

Here is a market. The blue line is demand: what buyers will pay for one more unit. Economists call it marginal benefit, MB = 100 - Q.

Firms supply along their own cost of one more unit, the private marginal cost PMC = 10 + Q. The market lands where the lines cross: 45 units at $55.

But every unit also pollutes. Neighbors suffer $20 of harm per unit, the marginal external cost (MEC). Stack it on top of PMC and you get the true cost to society, SMC.

Society wants output where the full cost meets the benefit: SMC = MB at Q* = 35.

The market ignores the $20 and makes 10 extra units. Each one costs society more than it is worth. The red triangle is the deadweight loss: ½ × 10 × 20 = $100.

The fix: a $20 tax on each unit. The firm now pays for the harm, so its cost line rises onto SMC, and the market picks 35 units on its own. The loss is gone. Some pollution remains: the efficient amount is not zero.

Make pollution more or less harmful, then switch the tax on and off.

Figure 11.2

Cap-and-trade: let the cheap cleaner do more

11.2 Cap-and-trade

Two factories must cut 100 units of pollution between them. For each firm, the more it cleans up, the more the next unit costs.

Firm A cleans up cheaply: its marginal abatement cost is MAC = 2e. Firm B is expensive: MAC = 8e.

Rule one: equal shares, 50 units each. A's last unit costs $100, but B's costs $400. Total bill: $12,500.

Move some cleanup from B to A. Each unit B drops saves more than it costs A to pick up. At 65 and 35 the bill falls to $9,125.

A permit market finishes the job. Firms trade permits until both face the same price, $160. Now A cuts 80 and B cuts 20.

Same 100 units, for $8,000 instead of $12,500: 36% cheaper. When every firm's last unit costs the same, the cleanup is as cheap as it can be.

Your turn

Split the 100 units yourself and hunt for the cheapest mix. Then let permits decide.

Figure 11.3

Spillover benefits and the subsidy

11.3 Pigouvian subsidy

Flu shots. The blue line is each buyer's own benefit from one more shot: MB = 100 - Q.

Sellers supply along marginal cost, MC = 10 + Q. The market settles at 45 shots for $55.

But your shot also protects the people around you: a marginal external benefit of $20 per shot. Add it on top to get society's benefit, MSB = MB + MEB.

Society wants shots up to where MSB meets MC: Q* = 55.

The market stops at 45. Shots 45 to 55 are worth more to society than they cost, yet never happen. The red triangle is the lost value: ½ × 10 × 20 = $100.

The fix: a $20 subsidy per shot. Buyers act as if the spillover were theirs, demand rises onto MSB, and the market makes 55 shots. Sellers get $65, buyers pay $45, and the loss is gone.

Your turn

Change how much each shot protects others, then switch the subsidy on and off.

Public goods and free riders

11.4 Free riding

A streetlight for the block costs $300. Once it is on, it lights the street for everyone, and nobody can be left in the dark.

Amara values the light at $120. That is less than $300, so she will not buy it alone.

Ben and Cy value it at $120 each too. Nobody will pay $300 by themselves.

But they all enjoy the same light, so stack their values: $120 + $120 + $120 = $360. That is vertical summation. It beats the cost by $60, so the light is worth building.

The catch: each neighbor waits for the others to pay, since they get the light either way. So nobody pays. The light is never built and the $60 is lost. That is free riding.

The fix: a deal where everyone pays $100 or nobody does, like a pledge drive or a local tax. Each gets $120 of light for $100, $20 better off, so all three sign up.

Your turn

Change how many neighbors share the light and how much each one values it.

Figure 11.5

The Coase theorem

11.5 Coase

A factory sits upstream from a neighbor. Each unit of pollution S saves the factory less than the last, MB = 100 - 2S, and hurts the neighbor more, MD = 2S.

The efficient level is S* = 25. There, the last unit is worth $50 to the factory and does $50 of harm.

Say the factory owns the right to pollute, so it starts at 50. Units 25 to 50 hurt the neighbor more than they help the factory: $1,250 of gains from a deal.

So the neighbor pays the factory to cut back to 25. Any payment from $625 to $1,875 leaves both better off.

Now flip it. The neighbor owns the right to clean air, so pollution starts at 0. Units 0 to 25 help the factory more than they hurt the neighbor.

This time the factory pays the neighbor to allow 25. Same end point either way. Who owns the right decides who pays, not how much gets polluted.

Your turn

Give the right to one side, then let them bargain. Bargaining assumes it is cheap to negotiate.
