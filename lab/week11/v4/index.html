Externalities and public goods

Figure 11.1

Pollution and the Pigouvian tax

A factory’s pollution does $20 of harm per unit to the people living downstream, a cost the factory never pays. Buyers value the Qth unit at $100 − Q, and making it costs the factory $10 + Q.

Now charge the factory a tax of $0 per unit.

The right tax equals the harm. It does not ban the activity; it makes the polluter pay the full cost of every unit.

Figure 11.2

Cap and trade

Two firms must together cut 100 tons of pollution. Cleanup gets harder the more a firm does: Firm A’s next ton costs $2 for each ton it has already cut, Firm B’s costs $8.

Split the work by /.

Trading makes the last ton cost the same at every firm, so the cheap cleaner does most of the work and the same target costs about a third less.

Figure 11.3

Flu shots and the Pigouvian subsidy

A flu shot protects the people around you too, a benefit worth $20 to them per shot. You value the Qth shot at $100 − Q, and supplying it costs $10 + Q.

Now pay a subsidy of $0 per shot.

A positive spillover is pollution in reverse: the market does too little, and the fix is a subsidy equal to the benefit others receive.

Figure 11.4

Public goods and free riders

3 neighbors could share a security guard who costs $300 a month. Each neighbor values the guard at $120 a month, and nobody can be kept from the protection once it is there.

For a public good, add everyone’s value on top of each other. Worth providing is not the same as provided: free riding leaves the gain on the table.

Figure 11.5

The Coase theorem

Each unit of pollution S is worth $100 − 2S to a factory and does $2S of damage to the neighbor next door. At 25 units both are $50, so 25 is the efficient level.

The right to the air belongs to /,

Striking a deal costs $0 in lawyers and meetings.

Who owns the right decides who pays, not where pollution ends up, as long as bargaining is cheap.
