EC224 Intermediate Microeconomics, Week 11

Externalities and public goods

Every graph on this page moves when you grab it. Drag a round handle and the curves, the shaded areas and the dollar figures follow your hand. When you reach a point that matters, the handle clicks into place and says why.

This is a handle. The arrows show which way it moves. On a keyboard, tab to it and use the arrow keys.

Figure 11.1

The Pigouvian tax

Buyers weigh their own benefit, MB = 100 − Q, against the firm's private cost, PMC = 10 + Q. But every unit also does $20 of harm to people outside the deal, so the true cost to society is SMC = PMC + $20. Left alone, the market makes too much.

Pull the gold point up the demand curve. The gap that opens between what buyers pay and what firms keep is the tax. Then drag the red handle to change the harm.

Figure 11.2

Cap-and-trade

Two firms each emit 100 units and each get 50 permits, so together they must clean up 100 units. Firm A cleans up cheaply, MAC = 2e. Firm B is expensive, MAC = 8e. Firm A's cleanup is measured from the left, Firm B's from the right, so every point on the axis is one way to split the 100 units.

Drag the split. Each step right means B buys one more permit from A, and A does one more unit of cleanup in its place.

Figure 11.3

A positive externality and the subsidy

Vaccines, schooling and research help people beyond the buyer. Buyers count only their own benefit, MB = 100 − Q, but each unit is worth another $20 to everyone else, so social benefit MSB = MB + $20 sits above demand. The market makes too little.

Drag the quantity along the axis and watch the surplus and the loss change shape. The gold gap is the subsidy it would take to get buyers there.

Figure 11.4

Public goods and free-riding

Three neighbours share a street. Streetlights cost $300 each, and nobody can be kept out of their light, so all three enjoy every light at once. That is why the group's value is the vertical sum of their curves: add up what each would pay for the same light.

Drag any neighbour's curve up or down and watch the black sum follow. Move the reading line along the axis to see the three values added up.

Figure 11.5

The Coase theorem

A factory gains from polluting, MB = 100 − 2S. Its neighbour is harmed, MD = 2S. If the right is clear and bargaining is cheap, the two trade their way to S* = 25 whoever holds the right. Only the direction of the payment changes.

Drag the bargaining point toward 25. Then tap the other side's label to hand them the right, and bargain again.
