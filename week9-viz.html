Section 1 · Pricing with Market Power
Fig 9.1

Two-Part Pricing Benchmark

A monopolist can capture the entire consumer-surplus triangle with a two-part tariff: a per-unit charge \(p\) plus a fixed access fee \(F\). Slide the usage charge. At \(p = MC = \$1\) the firm sells the efficient quantity and sets the fixed fee equal to the whole surplus, doubling uniform-monopoly profit and leaving zero deadweight loss. Raise \(p\) above marginal cost and both the surplus pie and the firm’s total profit shrink.

Per-unit charge p: $1.00
The doubling result. At \(p=MC\) the fixed fee sweeps up the entire consumer-surplus triangle, which under linear demand is exactly twice the uniform-monopoly profit rectangle. That is why two-part pricing doubles profit using only aggregate demand, and why raising (p) above (MC) only shrinks the pie.
Section 2 · Regulating a Natural Monopoly
Fig 9.2

Natural Monopoly: Four Regulatory Regimes

With large fixed cost and low marginal cost, average cost declines across the whole market, so \(MC < AC\) everywhere. Demand is \(P = 60 - Q\); the firm has \(TC = 200 + 5Q\). Switch between regimes to see the trade-off the regulator faces: the unregulated monopolist restricts output for profit, marginal-cost pricing is efficient but loses money, and average-cost pricing breaks even at a small efficiency cost.

The regulator’s dilemma. MC pricing is efficient but loss-making; AC pricing breaks even but leaves a small deadweight loss; the unregulated firm is profitable but least efficient. No single regime is simultaneously efficient and self-financing without extracting all consumer surplus, which is why most utilities are regulated at average cost.
Section 3 · Strategic Interaction
Fig 9.3

Game Theory: The Circle (Best-Response) Method

Every cell shows the payoffs (Player A, Player B). The circle (best-response) method finds the Nash equilibrium in two passes: for each thing B could do, find A’s best reply; for each thing A could do, find B’s best reply (the best replies are underlined on the grid). A cell where both players are best-responding at once is a Nash equilibrium, shaded green. Section 4 · Extracting More Surplus
Fig 9.4

Block Pricing: A Staircase Toward Perfect Discrimination

Instead of one price, a monopolist can charge a declining sequence of prices, a high rate for the first block of units and lower rates for later blocks (electricity and water tariffs work this way). Each extra block lets the firm claw back more of the consumer-surplus triangle. Slide the number of blocks: with one block the firm captures almost nothing, and as blocks multiply the staircase hugs the demand curve and profit approaches the perfect price-discrimination maximum. Demand \(P = 3 - Q/10\), \(MC = \$1\).

Number of price blocks: 3
More blocks, more surplus — and a limit. Block pricing is second-degree price discrimination: the firm never observes any individual’s willingness to pay, it just offers a menu of rates and lets buyers sort themselves. Each added block converts a slice of consumer surplus into profit and pushes output toward the efficient quantity, so second-degree pricing typically reduces deadweight loss. Now push the slider to the limit: if each block shrank to a single unit — one price per consumer’s unit — the staircase would sit exactly on the demand curve and capture the entire $20 surplus. That limiting case, one block per consumer, is first-degree (perfect) price discrimination. Block pricing is the practical, menu-based approximation to it.
