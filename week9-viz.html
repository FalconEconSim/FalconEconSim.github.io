Pricing with Market Power
§ 9.1

Two-Part Pricing Benchmark

A monopolist can capture the entire consumer-surplus triangle with a two-part tariff: a per-unit charge \(p\) plus a fixed access fee \(F\). Fig 9.1 below asks you to find the best usage charge; after the reveal you can slide it anywhere along demand. At \(p = MC = \$1\) the firm sells the efficient quantity and sets the fixed fee equal to the whole surplus, doubling uniform-monopoly profit and leaving zero deadweight loss. Raise \(p\) above marginal cost and both the surplus pie and the firm’s total profit shrink.

The doubling result. At \(p=MC\) the fixed fee sweeps up the entire consumer-surplus triangle, which under linear demand is exactly twice the uniform-monopoly profit rectangle. That is why two-part pricing doubles profit using only aggregate demand, and why raising \(p\) above \(MC\) only shrinks the pie.
Regulating a Natural Monopoly
§ 9.2

Natural Monopoly: Four Regulatory Regimes

With large fixed cost and low marginal cost, average cost declines across the whole market, so \(MC < AC\) everywhere. Demand is \(P = 60 - Q\); the firm has \(TC = 200 + 5Q\). In Fig 9.2 below, predict where the firm ends up left alone and under average-cost pricing; then switch between the four regimes to see the trade-off the regulator faces: the unregulated monopolist restricts output for profit, marginal-cost pricing is efficient but loses money, and average-cost pricing breaks even at a small efficiency cost.

The regulator’s dilemma. MC pricing is efficient but loss-making; AC pricing breaks even but leaves a small deadweight loss; the unregulated firm is profitable but least efficient. Only the two-part tariff is both efficient and self-financing, and it gets there by charging every customer an access fee: in the textbook version the fee takes the whole consumer surplus (here it is set just high enough to cover the $200 fixed cost). Because that flat fee is regressive and hard to set correctly, most utilities are regulated at average cost.

Game Theory: The Circle (Best-Response) Method

Every cell shows the payoffs (Player A, Player B). The circle (best-response) method finds the Nash equilibrium in two passes: for each thing B could do, find A’s best reply; for each thing A could do, find B’s best reply (the best replies are circled on the grid). A cell where both players are best-responding at once is a Nash equilibrium, shaded green. In Fig 9.3 below, circle the best replies yourself before you reveal the answer; afterwards, load a classic game or change any payoff and watch the equilibrium move, and Try a new one deals a fresh practice game.

How to read the grid. A blue circle marks Player A’s best response in that column; an orange circle marks Player B’s best response in that row. A green cell is where both are best-responding at once, a Nash equilibrium. A player has a dominant strategy when the same action is circled in both of that player’s cases. Use Try a new one to draw a fresh 2×2 game, circle the best responses yourself, then reveal the answer and the reasoning.
Extracting More Surplus
§ 9.4

Block Pricing: A Staircase Toward Perfect Discrimination

Instead of one price, a monopolist can charge a declining sequence of prices, a high rate for the first block of units and lower rates for later blocks (electricity and water tariffs work this way). Each extra block lets the firm claw back more of the consumer-surplus triangle. In Fig 9.4 below, price each block yourself; after the reveal, change the number of blocks: with one block, priced at marginal cost, the firm captures nothing, and as blocks multiply the staircase hugs the demand curve and profit approaches the perfect price-discrimination maximum. Demand \(P = 3 - Q/10\), \(MC = \$1\).

More blocks, more surplus, and a limit. Block pricing is second-degree price discrimination: the firm never observes any individual’s willingness to pay, it just offers a menu of rates and lets buyers sort themselves. Each added block converts a slice of consumer surplus into profit and pushes output toward the efficient quantity, so second-degree pricing typically reduces deadweight loss. Now take the number of blocks to the limit: if each block shrank to a single unit, one price for every unit, the staircase would sit exactly on the demand curve and capture the entire $20 surplus. That limiting case, one price per unit, is first-degree (perfect) price discrimination. Block pricing is the practical, menu-based approximation to it.
