Fig 4.1 Elasticity Meter: Point vs Arc

Drag either point along the demand curve. Point elasticity measures responsiveness at a single location using the formula e = −b·P/Q. Arc (midpoint) elasticity measures responsiveness between two points using the midpoint formula. Toggle between modes to compare how the two methods diverge as the gap between points widens.

Fig 4.2 Elasticity Heatmap: Living Demand Curve

The demand curve is shaded as a continuous gradient: red at the top (elastic, |e|>1), white at the midpoint (unit elastic, |e|=1), and blue at the bottom (inelastic, |e|<1). Drag the yellow dot to any point and read off its exact elasticity. Use the sliders to reshape the demand curve.

Intercept a: 100
Slope b: 2.0
Fig 4.3 Revenue Rectangle: Price vs Quantity Effect

Total revenue equals the area of the rectangle P×Q. When price rises, the green strip shows the revenue gained from existing buyers paying more; the red strip shows revenue lost from buyers who leave. The live verdict tells you which effect dominates.

New price P: $20

Select an industry with its real-world empirical elasticity estimate. Then adjust the price change and see the predicted change in total revenue. Uses the approximation %ΔTR ≈ %ΔP × (1 + ed).

Fig 4.5 Determinants Builder

Three key determinants of elasticity: number of substitutes, time horizon, and market breadth. Adjust each slider to see how the composite elasticity changes and how the demand curve reshapes. A broader market definition (e.g., "food" vs "pizza") and shorter time horizon both reduce elasticity.

Substitutes: 2
Time horizon: 2
Market breadth: 3
Fig 4.6 Consumer Surplus Triangle

Consumer surplus (CS) is the area of the triangle between the demand curve and the market price. Use the first slider to set the initial price, then raise it with the second slider to see how CS shrinks — decomposed into the rectangle (existing buyers now paying more) and the exit triangle (buyers priced out of the market).

Base price P*: $15
New price P': $25
Fig 4.7 Surge Pricing Simulator

Ride-share surge pricing in action. As the surge multiplier rises, rider demand falls, consumer surplus shrinks, and Uber's revenue grows — up to a point. Riders priced out exit the market entirely. This illustrates the welfare trade-off between producer revenue and consumer surplus.

Surge price: $5.00
Fig 4.8 Market Demand Builder: Horizontal Aggregation

The market demand curve is the horizontal sum of individual demand curves: at each price, add up every consumer's quantity demanded. Toggle consumers on/off and adjust their demand parameters. Notice how each consumer's choke price creates a kink in the market demand curve — where one buyer exits the market entirely.
