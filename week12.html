Adverse Selection & Moral Hazard

Prof. Naveen Sunder

Interactive Visualizations Homework
Note: This page is a structured summary of the Week 12 chapter, the course finale. It analyses information itself as a source of market failure: adverse selection (hidden type, before contracting) and moral hazard (hidden action, after contracting). The key diagnostic is timing, ex ante versus ex post.

Chapter Sections

Topics

Adverse selection arises when one party has private information about a fixed characteristic (quality, risk, type) that the other cannot observe before the transaction. Because buyers cannot tell high from low quality, they pay only the average expected value, which drives high-quality sellers out and leaves a disproportionate share of low quality: the word “adverse.”

In Akerlof’s lemons model (lemon: seller reserve $1,000, buyer WTP $1,200; peach: seller reserve $2,000, buyer WTP $2,400), the buyer pays \(\mathbb{E}[WTP]=1{,}200+1{,}200p\) where \(p\) is the peach fraction. Setting this equal to the peach seller’s reserve gives \(p^{*}=2/3\): above it the market pools (both trade), below it peaches exit and only lemons trade.

Akerlof’s Core InsightInformation asymmetry can destroy a market that would function perfectly under full information. The market for peaches fails not because peaches lack value ($400 gains from trade), but because buyers cannot distinguish them from lemons. Low quality imposes a negative externality on high-quality sellers through beliefs and prices.

With more than two quality tiers, the failure cascades. In each round buyers update their beliefs about the remaining pool, the expected value falls, and the next-highest tier finds the price unacceptable and exits. In a three-type market (Low 50%, Medium 30%, High 20%): at the start \(\mathbb{E}[WTP]=\$1{,}640\), below the High reserve of $3,000, so High exits; the pool re-weights, \(\mathbb{E}[WTP]\) falls to $1,150, below the Medium reserve, so Medium exits; only lemons remain.

The Deeper HarmAdverse selection is not just “more bad goods are traded.” In the extreme, complete unravelling means good goods stop being traded at all, even though buyers and sellers would both gain. The welfare loss is the destruction of high-quality markets, not mere mismatch.

Signalling is an action taken by the informed party to credibly communicate private information. It works only under the single-crossing condition: the marginal cost of the signal must be lower for high-quality types than for low-quality types, so only high types find it worth sending and low types cannot profitably mimic.

In Spence’s education model (\(y_H=\$100{,}000,\ y_L=\$40{,}000,\ c_H(s)=s,\ c_L(s)=3s\)), the two incentive-compatibility constraints give a separating range \(20\le s^{*}\le 60\). The minimum-cost (Riley) equilibrium is \(s^{*}=20\). Because education here adds no productivity, any \(s^{*}>0\) is pure social waste, showing how asymmetric information can cause over-investment in credentials.

Signalling Beyond EducationThe same single-crossing logic explains car warranties, brand reputation, professional certifications (CFA, CPA), and dividend policy. A signal is credible only when it is cheap enough for the high type but too expensive for the low type to copy. Whether it is socially efficient depends on whether the signal also raises productivity.

Screening is the mirror image of signalling: the uninformed party designs a menu of contracts that induces each type to self-select. The classic case is insurance with different deductibles: low-risk individuals prefer high-deductible/low-premium contracts; high-risk prefer low-deductible/high-premium.

Effective screening menus require distortions: the contract designed for the low-risk type must be made less attractive than the full-information optimum (partial coverage, higher deductibles) so high-risk types will not mimic. The low-risk type bears the cost of information asymmetry, the second-best outcome.

eBay and the Lemon ProblemWhen eBay launched in 1995, buyers could not inspect goods, a textbook adverse-selection setup. Its reputation/feedback system turned this into a repeated game: sellers with 100% positive feedback fetch 5–10% higher prices, exactly as the signalling premium predicts. Third-party certification (car inspections, credit ratings) does the same by making quality publicly verifiable.

Moral hazard arises when one party takes a hidden action (effort) after the contract is signed. An insured driver drives less carefully; a securitising bank under-screens loans. Because effort is unobservable, a flat salary produces zero effort: the agent bears the cost of effort but not its reward.

In the principal–agent model with a linear contract \(w=\alpha+\beta\pi\), the optimal performance weight is \(\beta^{*}=\dfrac{1}{1+r\sigma^{2}}\), where \(r\) is the agent’s risk aversion and \(\sigma^{2}\) the variance of the profit shock. Higher \(\beta\) sharpens incentives but loads more risk onto a risk-averse agent, the incentive–insurance trade-off. As \(r\) or \(\sigma^{2}\) rises, \(\beta^{*}\) falls toward a flatter, more insured contract.

Comparative StaticsWhen output is very noisy (\(\sigma^{2}\) large), pay reflects luck more than effort, so the optimal contract puts little weight on performance. When the agent is very risk-averse (\(r\) large), loading risk is costly, so \(\beta^{*}\) again falls. This is why CEO pay is more performance-linked in stable industries and salespeople face steep commissions where effort clearly drives sales.

The 2008 financial crisis combined both failures. Adverse selection: mortgage originators knew loan quality that MBS investors could not, and bundled the worse loans (Akerlof unravelling). Moral hazard: once a bank sold a loan into a security, it no longer bore default risk, so screening effort collapsed (“NINJA” loans). Dodd-Frank’s skin-in-the-game rule (originators retain 5%) directly targets the moral hazard.

Other applications: the health-insurance death spiral (healthy people exit a pooled premium, raising it, driving out the next-healthiest, hence the ACA individual mandate), and Stiglitz–Weiss credit rationing (raising the interest rate drives out safe borrowers, so banks ration credit rather than raise rates).

The Timing DiagnosticAdverse selection is ex ante: the hidden type exists before the deal. Moral hazard is ex post: the hidden action is chosen after the deal. A useful test: a lie detector (revealing type) fixes adverse selection; a hidden camera (revealing action) fixes moral hazard.

Quick Reference

Solutions to Information Problems

Signalling
The informed party acts. Credible only under single-crossing: the signal must be cheaper for high types. Education, warranties, brands.
Screening
The uninformed party offers a menu that induces self-selection. Requires distorting the low-type contract. Insurance deductibles.
Certification & Reputation
Third parties verify quality; repeated interaction gives sellers a stake in honesty. eBay feedback, credit ratings, inspections.
Performance Pay
Ties reward to observable output so effort is incentivised, trading incentives against insurance via \(\beta^{*}=1/(1+r\sigma^{2})\).

Exam Prep

Five Common Mistakes

Watch out for these

  • Confusing adverse selection with moral hazard. Adverse selection is a hidden type that exists before the deal; moral hazard is a hidden action chosen after the deal. Timing is the diagnostic.
  • Thinking adverse selection just means “more bad goods.” The deeper harm is that good goods stop trading entirely; complete unravelling can destroy the high-quality market.
  • Believing a signal works if it is merely costly. It must satisfy single-crossing: cheaper for the high type. If both types face the same cost, the signal cannot separate them.
  • Assuming higher performance pay is always better. A larger \(\beta\) sharpens incentives but loads risk onto a risk-averse agent. The optimum balances the two: \(\beta^{*}=1/(1+r\sigma^{2})\).
  • Claiming signalling is always socially wasteful. It is wasteful only if the signal adds no productivity. If education also raises skill, the signal has dual value and may be efficient.
