§ 7.1

Profit Landscape (TR vs TC)

The firm earns profit wherever TR > TC: profit is the vertical gap between the two curves. In Fig 7.1 below, find the output where that gap is widest. The plum bar shows the gap at your guess, and a dashed line parallel to TR can be pulled down until it just touches TC. After the reveal, change the price or the fixed cost and move the marker to compare MR with MC at any quantity: q* is where the slopes are equal (MR = MC).

Key insight: The firm maximizes profit where MR = MC (the revenue from one more unit exactly equals its cost). To the left of q*, MR > MC (produce more). To the right, MC > MR (cut back).

§ 7.3

Shutdown Decision Zone Map

In Fig 7.3 below, find the two prices that divide the zones: below the shutdown price the firm should shut down, between the two it operates at a loss, at the break-even price it breaks even, and above it the firm earns a profit. After the reveal, move the price P* through the zones. The algebraic proof of the shutdown rule never changes. What changes is the price, and therefore which zone (and which decision) applies.

Algebraic proof: when to produce. Produce if the operating loss is smaller than the shutdown loss:
  1. Profit > −TFC → keep producing
  2. TR − TVC − TFC > −TFC
  3. TR − TVC > 0, so TR > TVC
  4. P·q > AVC·q
Conclusion: P > AVC. Produce whenever price covers average variable cost; shut down otherwise.
§ 7.5

Supply Curve Emergence

Watch the price sweep upward from zero. Below the shutdown price (min(SAVC) = $4), the firm supplies nothing. Once price crosses $4, the SMC curve becomes the supply curve and each (q, P) point traces out in the right panel, building the supply curve dot by dot.

The core idea: how we get a supply curve out of a cost curve.
  1. Pick a price P.
  2. The firm maximizes profit by producing where P = SMC (the profit-maximizing rule).
  3. That intersection pins down a specific quantity q.
  4. Plot the point (q, P): that's one point on the firm's supply curve.
  5. Repeat for every price … and the dots you trace out land exactly on the SMC curve.
That's why the SMC curve (above the shutdown point) is the firm's supply curve: profit-maximization (P = SMC) directly maps each price to the quantity the firm will supply at that price.

§ 7.8

Market Supply Builder

Market supply is the horizontal sum of all individual firm supply curves: at each price, add up the quantities each firm wants to supply. In Fig 7.8 below, predict where market supply starts and how much the market supplies at one price; after the reveal, change the number of firms and move the price to see exactly how the arithmetic works.

Key insight: At each price, add quantities across all firms; never add prices. If each of n identical firms supplies q units, market supply is Q = n × q. The market supply curve is flatter than any individual firm's supply curve (each $1 rise in price adds n times as many units) and shifts right as n grows. With identical firms its price elasticity at any given price is the same as one firm's: flatter is not the same as more elastic.
§ 7.9

Long-Run Entry/Exit Machine

Each firm has the cost curves of Fig 7.8 (min SAC = $5.51 at q = 3.58), and market supply is n firms' SMC curves added together. Demand has just risen from D (Qd = 3,558.6 − 548.4P) to D′ (Qd = 4,086 − 548.4P), as in the animation. With 150 firms price is $6.40 and each firm earns $3.31 (green rectangle). New firms enter, market supply shifts right, the price falls, and the profit rectangle shrinks toward zero at long-run equilibrium (n ≈ 297, P* = $5.51 = minimum SAC, the flat LRS line). In Fig 7.9 below, predict that equilibrium and the number of firms; after the reveal, replay the entry or step the number of firms. An industry with too many firms (n = 450) runs the other way: price is $4.73, below SAC, the rectangle turns red (a loss of $2.69 per firm), and firms exit until price climbs back to the same zero-profit equilibrium at $5.51.

Zero economic profit is healthy: it means the firm earns exactly its opportunity cost. Owners are compensated fairly, just not earning excess returns.
