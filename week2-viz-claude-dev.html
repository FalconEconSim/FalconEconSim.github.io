Section 1 · Preferences
Fig 2.1The Indifference Map & a Falling MRS

For Cobb-Douglas preferences U = X0.5Y0.5, every curve is a set of bundles the consumer ranks equally; curves further from the origin are preferred. The marginal rate of substitution (MRS) is the slope of the tangent line, how many Y the consumer will give up for one more X. Slide the point along the middle curve and watch the MRS fall as X rises: diminishing MRS.

Bundle position (X): 6.0
Key insight: Diminishing MRS makes indifference curves bow toward the origin (convex). As you move down a curve, each extra X is worth fewer and fewer Y.
Section 2 · Constraints & Choice
Fig 2.2The Budget Constraint

The budget line PX·X + PY·Y = M is every bundle the consumer can just afford. Its intercepts are M/PX and M/PY; its slope is −PX/PY, the rate the market lets you trade X for Y. Move the sliders to see income shifts (parallel) versus price changes (pivots).

Income M: $120
Price of X: $6.0
Price of Y: $6.0
Read it: A change in M slides the line in and out keeping the slope; a change in a price spins the line around the unaffected axis intercept.
Fig 2.3The Consumer's Optimum (Tangency)

Put preferences and the budget together. The best affordable bundle sits where the budget line is tangent to the highest reachable indifference curve, the point where MRS = PX/PY. For Cobb-Douglas U = XαY1−α, that is X* = αM/PX, Y* = (1−α)M/PY. Change any slider and the tangency tracks it.

Income M: $120
Price of X: $6.0
Price of Y: $6.0
X-preference α: 0.50
The condition: At the optimum the curve and the line have the same slope: the consumer's willingness to trade (MRS) exactly equals the market's rate of exchange (the price ratio).
Fig 2.4Income & Price Changes

Start at the baseline optimum (shown as a faint ghost). Change income or the price of X and watch the new budget line, the new tangent indifference curve, and the new optimum. The horizontal jump in X* as PX changes is exactly what the demand curve records.

Income M: $120
Price of X: $6.0
From choice to demand: Drop PX and X* rises. Trace that (PX, X*) relationship and you have drawn the demand curve, the bridge into Week 3.
Section 3 · Consistency
Fig 2.5Why Indifference Curves Cannot Cross

Suppose two indifference curves crossed at point A. Then A and B lie on one curve (equally good), and A and C lie on the other (equally good), so by transitivity B and C must be equally good, yet C has more of both goods than B. That contradicts "more is better." Press play to walk the contradiction.

Conclusion: The only way to avoid the contradiction is that indifference curves never cross.
